codecast · product proposal · 2026-09-05 · draft for review

An organization
of agents, hung on the work you already track.

Layered agent roles that each own a scope of the work graph, wake on what changes inside it, and report upward as facts plus a short narrative. You read the company at whatever altitude you want, and answer only what nobody below you was allowed to decide.

surfaces: org, roles, inbox, projects, tasks, chat, decisions, mobile, CLIgrounded in: anchors, spawn, session_decisions, tasks, plans, projects, chat wakes
THE ONE PARAGRAPHCodecast already has every part of an organization except the middle. Sessions do the work. Tasks, plans and projects hold the work. Chat and the decision queue carry the conversation. One anchor stands by. What is missing is a layer of standing agents between the human and the sessions: a role for each project or plan, with a charter, a scope, a budget and a memory, that wakes when its scope changes, delegates to hands, answers what its charter allows, escalates the rest with a recommendation, and keeps a brief the layer above can read in ten seconds. The org chart is not a new tree. It is the project and plan tree with roles hung on its nodes, so it can never drift from the work.

This proposal covers the product end to end: the concepts, how they map onto what codecast does today, every surface they touch, the mechanics that make it cheap and trustworthy, the schema changes, the guardrails, and a four phase rollout with acceptance tests. It is written from first principles against the code, not against prior drafts.

What makes this different from "spawn more agents". Three commitments. First, roles hang on the existing work graph, so scope is never a second configuration surface. Second, every rollup separates facts the system computed from narrative the agent wrote, and every narrative line links to evidence. Third, the brief is the role's memory and the session is disposable, so a role survives compaction, model swaps, usage limits and restarts without losing the plot.

01Why now: the human is the integrator

A codecast user runs many sessions a day. Each one is a peer: it files under a personal label, binds to one task or plan, talks to other sessions with cast send, and settles into an inbox bucket that answers one question, who acts next. The inbox is excellent at that question. It is silent on every other one: what is the state of the Infrastructure project, which of these eleven sessions are pulling in different directions, what did we decide yesterday about the sync layer, what does the whole thing cost.

Today the only node that holds the whole picture is the human. Every card must be read, every decision answered, every collision noticed by them. The product has already grown the instruments that hint at the fix: a session's pinned state says what it is working on, the settle classifier says whether it is done, a project has an Updates tab that anticipates an agent digest, tasks have a human board and an internal board, an anchor stands by as a persistent member with a bot identity and a daily session cap, and hands nest under the session that spawned them. Each instrument works at one altitude. Nothing connects the altitudes.

The result is a familiar ceiling. Below about ten live sessions the human is a good manager. Above it they are a switchboard. The fix is not a better switchboard. It is the thing every organization invents when the founder can no longer hold everything: a layer of people whose job is to hold one part of it and to tell the founder only what the founder needs.

02The idea: an organization is a context hierarchy

Strip a company down and it is a device for managing context. The person at the top holds goals and a one line status per department. A department head holds their department's plan and a paragraph per project. A project lead holds the plan and knows every open thread. An engineer holds the diff. Each layer compresses what is below it and carries intent from above. Nobody reads everything, and the company still moves as one.

Agents need this more than people do, because an agent's context is a hard budget and it decays with every compaction. A standing agent that tries to hold a whole workspace in one transcript fails the same way a founder with forty reports fails. So the org is built as a context hierarchy first and a delegation hierarchy second.

Concretely:

  • A role owns a scope. A scope is a node in the work graph the product already has: the workspace, a project, or a plan. A role sees the facts of its scope, wakes on changes inside it, and writes inside it by default.
  • A role keeps a brief. One living document per role: goal, state, blockers, decisions, what happens next. The system writes the fact block, the role writes the narrative. The layer above reads briefs, not transcripts.
  • Roles report up and delegate down. Down: spawn hands, assign tasks to subordinate roles, write briefs for them. Up: a state line, a digest, and decisions that exceed the role's authority, each with a recommendation.
  • The human sits wherever they want. At the top by default. Or in a role's seat, when they want to lead a project themselves. The org does not care whether a seat is held by a person or an agent.

The payoff is a single new question the product can answer at any altitude: how is this going, and what do you need from me? Ask it of the workspace and the anchor answers in five lines. Ask it of a project and its lead answers with a brief. Ask it of a plan and you get the plan lead's view. Ask it of a session and you get today's inbox card. Same data, four altitudes, one gesture.

03Principles

1

The org is the work graph, not a second tree.

Roles hang on projects and plans. There is no separate org chart to keep in sync. Retire a project and its lead has nothing to hold; move a plan and its lead moves with it.

2

Facts computed, narrative written, every claim linked.

Task counts, hand states, open decisions, spend and staleness are computed by the server and injected. The role adds judgment on top. A narrative line without a link to a task, a diff, a message or a decision does not count as a report.

3

The brief is the memory. Sessions are disposable.

A role must be restartable from its charter, its brief and its scope facts with nothing lost that matters. That is the test for what belongs in the brief, and it is what makes model swaps, limit parks and compaction survivable.

4

Sleep by default, wake on events, coalesce.

No standing meetings, no polling. A role goes dormant at the end of every turn and is woken by changes inside its scope. Events within a short window fold into one wake with a digest. A quiet scope costs nothing.

5

Decide within charter, escalate with a recommendation.

Every role has an explicit list of what it may decide alone. Anything outside it moves up the chain. Nothing reaches the human without the intermediate roles' recommendations attached and the chain visible.

6

One identity per role, billed to a host.

A role is a bot member of the team. Its comments, chat posts, task assignments and briefs carry its name. The human host's account runs and pays for it, exactly as the anchor works today.

7

Blast radius is explicit and pausable.

Daily caps on hands, wakes and tokens per role. Writes fenced to scope. A subtree can be paused with one gesture and everything under it goes quiet until resumed.

8

Reuse the rails.

Wakes are pending messages. Hands are spawns. Reports are project updates and thread states. Escalations are session decisions. Charters and briefs are docs. Identity is the anchor's bot user. The org adds one table and a handful of fields, not a new runtime.

06The shape of an org

Shapes are not prescribed. The rule is that a role's scope must sit inside its parent's scope, and depth is capped at three roles below the root. Hands are leaves and do not count. Two shapes cover almost everyone.

A small team's org. Left stripe on each node is who acts next: red the human, blue an agent, green nobody, grey dormant. The same stripe the inbox uses.

Shape one: a solo builder

The anchor, one lead per active project, hands. Three altitudes. The human talks to the anchor in the morning and to a lead when they want to go deep. This is the shape cast org init proposes by default: it reads the active projects and offers one lead each, with charters drafted from the project's description and its recent updates.

Shape two: a small team

A team anchor, project leads, and plan leads under the busiest projects. A human product manager may hold a lead seat; the agent leads below them report to a person, and the person's inbox is where their escalations land. Humans in seats are first class: the chart shows a dashed node, and the wake frame of a subordinate says "you report to Sam" instead of naming a role.

Boundaries the shapes respect

A workspace has one org. A personal workspace and a team workspace are separate trees with separate anchors, the same boundary tasks, plans and docs already draw with their workspace key. Work that belongs to no project, which is most of a personal workspace's tasks, sits in the root scope and belongs to the anchor. A plan carries one project, so a plan lead always sits under exactly one project lead, or under the anchor when the plan has no project. Nothing about this is new: it is where those rows already live.

What is not a shape

Peers without a parent, roles scoped to a label, roles scoped to a repo path, roles that report to two parents. Each of these is a real request and each is explicitly deferred in section 13 so that the first version has one scope rule and one chain per decision.

08Surfaces

The org touches nine surfaces. Each gets the smallest change that makes the org visible there, and each reuses the component that already renders the underlying object.

8.1 The org page and the altitude control

A new route, /org, is the flagship. It renders the work graph with roles on its nodes and an altitude control at the top. The control is the interaction the whole proposal exists to deliver.

  • Board. One line per top level scope, written by that scope's role, with the stripe and the evidence links. Fits on a phone.
  • Lead. One brief per role: goal, state, blockers, decisions, next, hands. The chart is drawn at this altitude, as in section 6.
  • Floor. Session cards, exactly as the inbox renders them, grouped under their role. This is today's inbox with structure.

Node stripes reuse the inbox's work state colors, so the chart doubles as a heat map of attention. A node's actions: open the role, talk to it, pause the subtree, add a role beneath it. Adding a role is a two field form: pick a scope from the projects and plans under the parent, name it. Charter and brief are drafted for review.

The page also shows two totals that keep the org honest: spend today by role, and wakes today by role. A role that wakes fifty times a day is misconfigured, and the number is where the human sees it.

8.2 The role page

A role's home, at /org/<handle>. From top to bottom:

  1. The brief. Fact block on the left (computed, with an "as of" stamp and a count of scope events since), narrative on the right (the role's words, each line linked). Stale briefs render greyed the way a stale thread state does.
  2. Talk to it. A composer that delivers into the standing session. The transcript is embedded below it using the existing conversation view, so the role page is also the session page.
  3. Hands. Session cards nested under the role, live, with the same gestures as the inbox.
  4. Decisions. Three lists: raised by this role, answered by this role on behalf of its hands, escalated upward. The middle list is the audit of delegated authority.
  5. Routines. The triggers this role owns, with pause and run.
  6. Charter. The doc, editable inline by humans. A change is delivered to the role as a wake so it re-reads its instructions.
  7. Settings. Model and backend, host, budget caps, authority list, status.

8.3 The inbox

Roles are hidden from the inbox unless hard blocked, the rule that already applies to the anchor. Hands nest under their role, using the nesting the sidebar already does for subagents and teammates. Two additions:

  • An Org section at the top of the sidebar lists roles that need the human, in the same card shape, with the role identity in the chip. Empty when no role is blocked, which is the normal state.
  • The Questions bucket shows a decision's chain on the card, so the human knows at a glance it already passed a lead.

8.4 Projects, plans and tasks

Project page. The header gains the lead's chip and the first line of its brief, with a "talk to the lead" action that opens the role composer in a side panel. The Updates tab is where the lead posts digests, badged as digests as the tab already does. The Tasks tab gains a "delegated to" facet so the human can see which tasks a role has handed to hands or to subordinate roles.

Plan page. A plan with a lead shows the lead's chip and brief line in the plan header. The plan's timeline entries written by the lead carry its identity.

Tasks. A role handle is a valid assignee. Assigning to a role is delegation: it wakes the role with the task in its frame. A task assigned to a role stays off the human board until a human promotes it, which keeps the board predicate's promise that an assignee means a person is on the hook. The task detail shows the owning role of the project and any hand bound to the task. cast task ready inside a role's session is scoped to the role by default.

8.5 Chat

Every role is a bot member with a handle. Mentioning it wakes it with the thread excerpt, exactly as the anchor wake works, and the same silence rules apply: a role posts once, in the place it belongs, and passes on lines not meant for it. A channel can be bound to a role's scope so that an unaddressed line in that channel goes to the project's lead rather than the anchor. Posts by a role carry its scope as a small badge so a reader knows which altitude is speaking.

8.6 Decisions

The decision card and the queue gain a chain. A card shows who raised it, who it passed through, what each of them recommended, and who is being asked now. The options keep the order the asker gave them; the recommendation is marked, never moved to the top, so the human still reads the question. Answering a card sends the answer straight to the session that asked, and every role in the chain learns it in its next frame. When a role answers a decision within its authority the card never reaches the queue; it appears in the role page's audit list and in the day's digest so nothing is decided invisibly.

Recommendations also feed a number the product can act on: how often the human agreed with a role, per decision category. The role page shows it, and when a role has been agreed with every time in a category for long enough, the page offers to grant that category. This is how authority grows: earned in the open, granted by a person, revocable in one click. The same number falling is the early sign that a role's charter needs work.

8.7 Mobile

Mobile gets board altitude and the queue, and nothing else in the first version. The org page at board altitude is the morning page: three to eight lines, each with a stripe, and a count of decisions. Tapping a line opens the lead's brief. That is the whole mobile surface, and it is the one that changes the daily habit.

09Mechanics

The surfaces are only as good as the machinery underneath. This section is the engineering design: what wakes a role, what it is told, how it delegates, what it may do, how it remembers, what it may spend, and how the org stays out of its own way.

9.1 Wakes

A role sleeps. Its standing session is persistent and dormant, exempt from auto completion as the anchor is. A wake is one pending message delivered into that session, the same rail every trigger, chat mention and cast send already uses. What is new is the source of wakes and the coalescing in front of them.

Coalescing

Events that do not bypass are folded into a pending wake for the role. The window is short by default and configurable per role. When the window closes, one wake is delivered whose frame lists every folded event. If a bypassing event arrives while a window is open, the window closes immediately and the bypassing event rides the same wake. If the role's session is mid turn, the wake waits for the turn to end; the frame is generated at delivery time, not at enqueue time, so it is never stale.

The loop breaker. A wake whose frame contains no new fact since the previous frame is dropped, not delivered. A role's own writes (its brief, its comments, its task updates) never wake it. A sibling role's message wakes it, but the resulting frame is checked against the same rule. This is what stops two leads from waking each other forever with acknowledgements.

9.2 The wake frame

The anchor today gets one bootstrap briefing at provisioning and relies on its own memory after that. That is too fragile for a role that will be restarted, swapped and parked. Instead, every wake carries a frame, generated by the server at delivery time, that a fresh session could act on with nothing else in context. The frame has five parts, in this order:

  1. Who you are. Role name, handle, scope, who you report to (a role or a person), who reports to you. Two lines.
  2. Why you are awake. The events since the last wake, as a list of facts with ids: task ct-49001 moved to done by hand jx7…, decision d-211 answered by Ashot with option 2, mention in #infra by Sam. Bypassing events first.
  3. Your scope right now. The fact block: tasks by status and priority, plans with progress, live hands with their state lines, open decisions, unresolved comments, spend and wakes today against the caps, the brief's age.
  4. What your subordinates say. The first line of each subordinate role's brief and each hand's pinned state. Never their transcripts.
  5. Your charter. Verbatim, from the doc. Last, so the model reads the situation before the rules.

The frame is the only per role prompt. The machine wide instruction snippets that teach every session the cast commands stay as they are, installed per instruction file. Nothing about the org lives in a static file that could drift from the row.

The frame has a size budget. The fact block truncates by priority and says so. A role whose scope is too big for its frame is a role whose scope should be split, and the org page flags it.

9.3 Delegation and escalation

Down the chain there are three moves, all existing verbs with a role identity attached:

  • Start a hand. cast spawn --as <role> creates a session with role_id set, nested under the standing session, in a worktree when the scope has a repo. The hand's first turn is the brief the role writes. The hand inherits the role's scope for writes and its host for billing. The precedent already exists: a session spawned for a task takes its parent from the task's plan today, so a plan is already a thing sessions hang under.
  • Assign a task to a subordinate role. Assignment is delegation. The subordinate wakes with the task in its frame and claims it as itself.
  • Write a brief for a subordinate. A parent may append to a subordinate's brief narrative, marked as the parent's words. This is how intent flows down without a chat message.

Up the chain there is one move: a decision. A hand that runs cast decide under a role no longer posts to the human's queue. It posts to its role. The role wakes, and its charter's authority list decides what happens:

  1. If the decision is inside the role's authority, the role answers it. The answer is recorded with resolved_by_role_id, appears in the role page's audit list and in the day's digest, and the hand resumes.
  2. If it is outside the authority, the role runs cast decide --escalate with its recommendation. The decision's chain grows by one step and it routes to the role's parent, which repeats the rule. At the human it lands in the queue with the whole chain.
  3. If the role cannot tell, it escalates. Ambiguity resolves upward, never by guessing.

A human's answer does not travel back down hop by hop. It is delivered straight to the session that asked, exactly as an answered decision reaches its session today, so the hand resumes within one delivery. Every role in the chain gets the answer as a fact in its next frame, so the lead learns what was decided without spending a turn relaying it. Advisory decisions work the same way, with the hand proceeding on its default in the meantime. The chain adds latency only on the way up, one turn per role, and section 9.1's bypass rule makes each of those turns immediate.

Where a root role escalates to is a setting, not a guess. A personal workspace's anchor escalates to its owner. A team anchor escalates to the role's owner set, which defaults to the team admins and can be narrowed to specific people from the role page. A subordinate role never needs the setting: its parent is a role or a person in a seat, and the chain follows that.

9.4 Scope and authority

Scope is enforced on writes and defaulted on reads, the same rule the workspace layer already follows. Access itself does not change: a role's bot user is a team member with the same workspace key as everyone else. Scope is a narrower fence inside that access, applied to what a role and its hands may change.

  • A role and its hands may create and update tasks, plans, docs and project updates inside the scope. Writes outside it are refused with a message naming the scope, and the refusal is a fact in the role's next frame so it can escalate.
  • A role may start hands in its scope and roles beneath it whose scope sits inside its own. It may not create, pause or retire a role above or beside it.
  • A role may post in chat channels bound to its scope, and reply where it was mentioned. It may not start threads in unrelated channels.
  • Reads default to scope so frames stay small, but a role may read anything its workspace allows. It is a colleague, not a prisoner.

Authority is a structured list on the role, written by humans in the charter's settings, not free text: which decision categories it may answer alone. The first version ships with a small fixed vocabulary of categories that decisions are tagged with when raised: implementation choice, test strategy, scope trim, schedule, spend under a limit, anything touching production or billing or deletion. A role's authority is a subset. The human sets it in the role's settings and can see, in the audit list, whether the role is using it well.

9.5 Memory and restart

Principle 3 is the design's strongest claim, so it needs a mechanism and a test.

The brief is a doc. Its fact block is not stored: it is computed on read from the same query that builds the wake frame, and cached against a version number that every write inside the scope bumps. A quiet scope costs nothing and a busy one costs one query per read, never one per write. Its narrative is written by the role with cast brief edit, and a role's charter tells it to do so at the end of any turn that changed its understanding. The brief's first line is mirrored into the standing session's thread state, so the inbox card, the org chart node and the brief never disagree.

Restart is a first class verb: cast role restart <handle> kills the standing session, starts a fresh one, and delivers a wake whose frame carries the brief in full instead of the first line. The acceptance test for the whole feature is that a role restarted this way gives the same answer to "how is this going and what do you need" as it gave before the restart. If it does not, the brief is missing something and the charter is wrong.

Model and backend swaps are the same operation. Usage limit parks are handled as they are for any session; the parent's frame shows the role as parked, and a parent may reassign the host with one setting change.

9.6 Budgets

Three caps per role, daily, with defaults inherited from the parent: hands started, wakes delivered, tokens spent. The anchor already has the first. A role at a cap goes dormant with a reason, surfaces once in the Org section of the parent and the human, and its coalescer keeps folding events for the next day. Spend is aggregated from session metrics per role and per subtree and shown on the org page and in the anchor's evening summary. The human can raise a cap from the role page or from the card.

9.7 Conflicts

Two hands in one scope touching the same file is the common failure of parallel agent work and the reason many people stop at one session. The org handles it in three layers:

  • Prevent. Hands are started in worktrees by default when the scope has a repo, using the workspace command that already allocates ports and copies env.
  • Detect. Overlapping file touches among live hands in one scope are a fact in the lead's frame, and among leads in the anchor's frame.
  • Resolve. The lead decides: pause one hand, sequence them, or merge. It is a judgment call, so it is the role's job, not a rule.

9.8 Rituals

An organization has a rhythm, and the org gets a small one by default, all implemented as triggers the roles own: an evening digest per lead into the project's Updates tab, an anchor summary into the team channel after the leads have posted, and a weekly brief review where each lead rewrites its brief from scratch against the facts. Every ritual is a trigger the human can see, pause or reschedule from the role page. A ritual that produced nothing new posts nothing, because a digest with no events is a fact in the frame and the charter says silence is the right answer to it.

12Rollout

Four phases. Each ships something a person can use, and each has an acceptance test that is a scenario, not a checklist.

Phase 1 · Roles existabout two weeks

The roles table with the anchor migration. cast role create/ls/show/pause/resume/retire/wake. Project scope only. The wake frame generator and the coalescer with task and hand transitions as sources. Hands nest under roles. Charter and brief docs. The org page at lead altitude. The inbox Org section.

Accept when: a lead created for a real project wakes when a task in it is marked done, starts a hand for the next ready task, and the hand nests under it in the sidebar. Restarting the lead with cast role restart loses nothing a human can detect.

Phase 2 · The chainabout two weeks

Decision routing with authority categories, chain rendering on the card and the queue, the audit list on the role page. Chat mentions per role and channel binding. Budgets with the three caps. Board altitude. Evening digests as a default ritual.

Accept when: a decision raised by a hand is answered by its lead without reaching the human, appears in the audit list, and a second decision in a forbidden category reaches the human with the lead's recommendation attached. The human answers from the phone and the hand resumes.

Phase 3 · Depth and safetyabout three weeks

Plan scope and plan leads. Worktrees by default for hands, file touch overlap in frames. Pause subtree with cascade. Frame budget and the scope too big flag. Humans in seats. cast org init proposing an org from active projects. Floor altitude. Mobile board altitude.

Accept when: two hands under one lead touching the same file produce a fact in the lead's frame and the lead resolves it without a human. A paused subtree produces zero wakes for a day and resumes with one.

Phase 4 · Polish until it disappearsongoing

Wake latency under ten seconds end to end. Spend by role in the anchor's summary. Charters from skills. Org templates for the two shapes. The weekly brief rewrite ritual. Frame size tuning by model. The details that make the morning page the first thing opened.

Accept when: the human's daily touches on a workspace with three leads and ten hands are a page read, the decisions in the queue, and one evening message, for five working days in a row.

13What we deliberately do not build

  • A separate org editor. Roles are created from scopes. There is no drag and drop chart that could disagree with the projects list.
  • Roles for labels or paths. A label is personal filing and a path is a machine detail. Both are real requests for a later version, once scope has proven itself on projects and plans.
  • Peer negotiation. Sibling roles may message each other, but conflicts between them are resolved by their parent. No consensus protocols.
  • Free text authority. Authority is a fixed vocabulary of categories. A role never interprets prose to decide whether it may decide.
  • Roles that run on their own account. A role is hosted by a human's daemon and account, as the anchor is. Independent agent accounts are an infrastructure question, not an org question.
  • Long transcripts as memory. The brief is the memory. A role that needs its transcript to function is a role whose brief is wrong.

14Decisions for the founder

Should the anchor keep its name, or become "the workspace role"?
Recommendation: keep it. It is a good word for the root, users know it, and the CLI keeps working. The org page labels it "Anchor · workspace".
Should a task assigned to a role stay off the human board?
Recommendation: yes. The board's promise is that an assignee is a person on the hook. A role's tasks are agent internal until a human promotes them, exactly as agent filed tasks are today.
Coalescing window default?
Recommendation: two minutes, with human messages, escalations and blocked hands bypassing. Short enough to feel live, long enough that a burst of task closes is one wake.
Who may grant authority categories to a role?
Recommendation: the host, the personal owner, or a team admin, the same gate that may reshape an anchor today. Never a role.
Should humans in seats be in phase 1 or phase 3?
Recommendation: phase 3. It is right and it is not what proves the idea. Phase 1 proves that a lead over a project makes the human's day shorter.

A.3 Coalescing rules

  1. An event inside a role's scope opens a window if none is open, of the role's coalesce_ms.
  2. Further events fold into the open window.
  3. A bypassing event closes the window at once.
  4. On close, the frame is generated. If it contains no fact newer than the previous delivered frame, the wake is logged as dropped and nothing is delivered.
  5. If the standing session is mid turn, delivery waits for turn end and the frame is regenerated then.
  6. If the role is paused or over a cap, the window stays open until resume or the next day, then delivers once.
  7. The role's own writes never open or fold into a window.
