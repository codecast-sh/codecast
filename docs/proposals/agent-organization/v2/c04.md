## 4. The human experience

Six surfaces make the organization usable: the morning brief, the tree, the decision card, chat, the hire flow and the health page. Each section below is one of them, with a mockup, the interactions it must support, and the details that make it feel finished rather than merely present. Mockups use this workspace's real project names and invented events.

### The morning brief

The workspace brief is the front page of codecast for anyone who has an anchor. It replaces nothing. The inbox is one keystroke away. But it is what you see first.

<!-- MOCKUP:brief -->

Details that matter:

- **Decisions first, and they are real controls.** The cards at the top are the same decision cards as the queue. Answering one here answers it everywhere. The card shows the ladder it climbed. Nothing is a summary of a decision; it is the decision.
- **Handled without you is the second thing you read.** Every decision a lead answered, every stall it fixed, every dead worker it replaced. Each carries <kbd>d</kbd>, disagree, which records an override against the lead. Items that can still be acted on also carry <kbd>r</kbd>, reopen, which puts the decision back in your hands and tells the worker. The count says how many cards you did not see.
- **Every line is a link.** Project names open the project's brief. Ids open their objects. The cost line opens the ledger. There is no sentence in the brief the reader cannot verify in one click.
- **Diff since you last read.** The brief remembers each reader's last read version, so "show changes" marks what changed for you, not since the previous version.
- **Coverage is always visible.** The footer says how many sessions and tasks the brief covers, how many it could not read, and how many claims were dropped for this reader. Never a bare green check.
- **Delivery.** At cadence time the workspace lead sends each responsible human one direct message with the "Needs you" count and the headline, using the anchor's existing direct message verb; on mobile that arrives as a push. A brief that is not ready by its cadence shows the previous version with its watermark and a note.
- **Keyboard.** <kbd>j</kbd> and <kbd>k</kbd> move between lines. <kbd>Enter</kbd> zooms into the referenced scope. On a decision card <kbd>1</kbd> to <kbd>3</kbd> select an option and <kbd>Enter</kbd> submits; a protected class asks once more. <kbd>i</kbd> jumps to the inbox. All keys render through the existing keycap component.

### For several humans

A workspace belongs to a team, so the brief has several readers and the ladder has several tops.

- **Every scope has a responsible human.** Plans already have an owner; projects gain one, set at hire time for a scope with a lead and editable on the page. A task's responsible human is its assignee, else its plan's owner, else its project's owner. A decision a lead asks for itself is held by its scope's responsible human, not by the lead's host.
- **Decisions climb to the responsible human of the asker's task.** "Needs you" is per reader: Ashot's brief shows "Needs you" and, below it, "Needs Maya". The responsible human, the scope's owner, a team admin, or a lead with a grant may answer. The first answer to commit wins and the other party sees who answered, as the decision record already does today.
- **The brief is one document, rendered per reader.** Chapter 3 defines the per claim audience. Two people reading the same project brief may see different lines and different coverage.
- **Grants belong to the scope.** The grant control renders only for the scope's owner or a team admin; agreements and overrides count from any human permitted to answer. Hiring, pausing and retiring a lead are the scope owner's or an admin's.
- **The first line of a team brief names its readers.** "For Ashot and Maya" is written by the lead from the scope's owners and assignees, so nobody wonders whether a line addressed to "you" means them.

### Zoom: the organization as a map

The organization view is a tree you can walk. It replaces neither the inbox nor the task board; it is the third way in, organized by scope rather than by time or by status. It grows from the existing fleet board, which already shows agents by plan and wave.

<!-- MOCKUP:zoom -->

Details that matter:

- **Each row is a brief headline.** A lead's row shows the first line of its brief, its next actor, its freshness and the count of things at that level that need a human. A worker's row shows its state line, exactly as the inbox does today.
- **Expand to zoom, select to read.** Expanding a row reveals its children. Selecting a row shows its full brief in the right pane. A worker's pane is the conversation, unchanged from today.
- **The human's obligations escape every fold.** A collapsed project that contains a decision for you still shows a red count on the project row. Collapsing is a display choice; it never hides an ask. The inbox already lifts a parent into the questions bucket when a child has an open decision, and the tree uses the same rule.
- **Missing levels are drawn, not hidden.** A project with no lead appears with a dotted outline and its tasks and sessions listed raw. The control to hire a lead is on that row, and nowhere else.
- **Flat view is one key away.** <kbd>f</kbd> toggles between the tree and the familiar flat session list filtered to the same scope. Nobody is forced to think in trees.
- **Drag to refile.** Dragging a task or plan onto another scope refiles it, using the existing task and plan update mutations. The drop preview shows the new reporting line before the drop. A move between lead scopes is a human action: the server refuses it from a role credential and from the task's own claimant session, so a worker cannot shop for a lead with a friendlier grant. The pilot refuses moves while a worker is running, with the reason, and the reporting chip shows a pending move.

### Decisions with a recommendation attached

The decision card is the moment where the organization earns or loses trust, so it is the most carefully designed surface in the proposal.

<!-- MOCKUP:decision -->

Details that matter:

- **The ladder is shown.** Who asked, who recommended, who concurred, and when. A recommendation from a lead is attributed to that lead by name, never presented as the system's view.
- **The recommended option is marked but not preselected.** The human still chooses. One keystroke selects the recommendation; a different keystroke selects something else. Neither is the default. No option is styled as primary.
- **The delegation control is on the card.** Below the options: "Let the Codecast lead answer questions like this." It names the lead that originated the recommendation and shows the agreement count. Choosing it grants the lead that decision class within that scope, with an undo. This is how trust grows, on the card, at the moment the human has just seen the lead be right. Chapter 5 defines the counting.
- **Protected classes have no delegation control**, show the bound operation, target and revision, and ask for a second confirmation. The leads' recommendations still appear, as evidence.
- **Overrides are recorded.** When the human picks against a recommendation, the card asks for one line of why, optional, and records the disagreement against the lead that made it. That record is visible on the lead's page.

### Talking to a lead

A lead is a session, so everything that works with a session works with a lead: `cast send`, chat mentions, `cast read`. The proposal adds one thing: leads are addressable by scope, not only by session id.

<!-- MOCKUP:chat -->

Details that matter:

- **Mention by scope.** In a channel bound to a project, `@lead` reaches the project lead. In an unbound channel, `@lead` reaches the workspace lead. `@lead codecast` reaches a named project's lead from anywhere. The existing channel to anchor mapping and mention machinery route it. A channel can be bound to a lead only if the lead's bot user is a member of it.
- **Answers from the brief first, live state second.** A lead answers a question from its brief when the brief covers it, and says "from my brief, written 08:34". When it does not, it reads live task and session state and says so. It never invents a state it did not read. Its answers in a channel are filtered to what that channel's members may read.
- **Intake is a proposal, not an action.** "Can we fix the duplicate notifications?" in a project channel produces a proposed task in the thread: title, plan, owner, deliverable, exclusions, estimated cost. Nothing exists until a human confirms. "Create only" needs task write access in the project. "Create and start" needs the scope's responsible human, or a member the owner has delegated budget to, and the created operation is keyed to the thread message so a second click creates nothing.
- **A lead can say "not mine".** A question about another project's work gets a pointer to that project's lead, or to the human if there is none. Leads do not answer for scopes they do not read.

### Hiring a lead

"Add a lead" is the only way a lead comes to exist. It is a short flow and it is deliberately the same flow for every scope.

<!-- MOCKUP:hire -->

Details that matter:

- **Scope is chosen, then shown.** The flow previews exactly which plans, tasks and sessions the lead will read, and how many it cannot read. The human sees the reading contract before agreeing to it.
- **Responsible human, host and payer.** The flow names the scope's owner, the machine or cloud host the lead runs on, and the account that pays. It shows whether that host can issue the per session credential and run the safe mode from chapter 8, which caps the stage. If the host is offline, the brief goes stale with a note and asks fall through to the human.
- **The standing brief is a text box with a good default.** Codecast proposes a job description from the project's name, its recent activity and its chat channel. The human edits it. It is the lead's instructions and it is theirs.
- **Trust starts at understand.** The first screen offers only the first stage: keep a brief, answer questions in chat, recommend on decisions. Decide and direct are visible, grayed, with the sentence "unlocks from the decision card as you agree with this lead's recommendations".
- **Caps and rhythm.** At understand the flow asks for a concurrency cap, a daily turn cap and an hourly wake cap, because that is what bounds a lead before dollar metering exists (chapter 5). The dollar budget is recorded here if the human wants, and required when direct unlocks. Nothing is prefilled. Rhythm is one choice: a time of day, or continuous with the coalescing default from chapter 8.
- **A lead can be paused, refreshed or retired from the same page.** Pause stops its wakes and keeps its brief. Refresh replaces its session from its documents. Retire keeps its history readable forever.

### The first brief

Within 10 minutes of hire, the lead writes its first brief in the six part shape. Version one's first 10 minutes scenario is the acceptance test. Its three honesty cases are the checks. An empty project yields an intake draft, not invented work. Inaccessible work yields "I could not verify", not a guess. A project with no blocker gets no manufactured task. A lead that creates a task to show activity fails the pilot.

### Reviewing work

A worker's completion is a claim. The organization turns that claim into a verified result without the human reading the diff, unless they want to. Three words stay distinct on every surface: claimed, verified, accepted.

- A worker runs `cast task done` with evidence, as today. The task moves to review.
- A human sets the review policy on the plan or the task. The default is independent review for anything touching schema, production, auth, billing or data, and a lead's own check for the rest.
- An independent reviewer is a fresh session provisioned from a different delegation root than the worker's lead. At direct stage the lead above provisions it against its own budget; otherwise the human control path does, which in the common starting shape of a project lead with no plan leads means the human. Version one allowed only the human control path; chapter 12 records the change. The reviewer gets the task, the acceptance criteria and the diff, reproduces the check, and writes a verdict. Only its check is "verified". Reviewer and critic cost is charged to the reviewed scope and shows in its cost section.
- Where the policy allows the lead's own check, the lead reads the evidence and reproduces what it can. The brief calls that "checked by the lead".
- The lead closes the task on a verdict, or sends it back with the unmet criteria. The server enforces this: a close from a role credential is refused unless a verdict matches the task's review policy. The server sets the default policy from the paths the diff touches and the task's effect class; a lead may tighten it and never loosen it; a human may set it either way. The human's acceptance, where the plan asks for it, is a third step and a third word.

### The lead's page

Every lead has a page. It is where the standing brief is edited, where grants live, and where the human sees what a lead has been.

<!-- MOCKUP:lead -->

Details that matter:

- **The standing brief is editable in place**, with the version the current session has acknowledged shown beside it.
- **Scope notes are readable and editable.** A human's edit wins over the lead's.
- **Grants sit with the card each came from**, its expiry, and renew and revoke controls. Only the scope's owner or an admin sees the controls.
- **Overrides are listed with their reasons**, oldest to newest, so a pattern is visible before the second consecutive one revokes.
- **Session history shows each replacement** with the reason and the one line reconstruction note.
- **Pause, refresh and retire** are here, and only here.

The project page carries a small panel that shows the lead's headline, next actor, freshness and a link to this page. The panel is not a second control surface.

### Organization health

A page the human reads once a week.

<!-- MOCKUP:health -->

Details that matter:

- **Track record per lead.** Decisions recommended on and answered. How often the human agreed. Overrides with their reasons. Claims sampled against their sources. This is the number the delegation control asks the human to trust.
- **Load.** Wake backlog, brief freshness, workers per lead, decisions waiting and their age. A lead with a growing backlog is visible before it becomes a problem.
- **Cost by scope**, with the lead's own line and the fraction that could not be priced.
- **Grants**, each with the card it was granted from, its expiry, and a revoke control.
- **Stalls the organization noticed** and what it did about them.

### Mobile

The mobile app renders the inbox and session transcripts today. It syncs decisions but has no screen that shows or answers one. The organization adds three screens and changes none. The mobile meta line names the same readers as the desktop brief.

<!-- MOCKUP:mobile -->

- The workspace brief, readable, with its handled section and its decision cards.
- A decision card that can answer: options with equal weight, the recommendation as a mark, a second confirmation on a protected class. This is new stage one work, listed in chapter 11.
- The organization tree, collapsible, with the same fold rule: your obligations show through.

Hiring a lead and editing its standing brief stay on desktop. Pausing a lead, answering its decisions, and, at stage three, stopping its running workers with a preview and taking over a review, do not. Version one required mobile intervention before managed work, and this version keeps that in the stage three exit.

### What the inbox gains and what it keeps

Nothing in this chapter removes a bucket, a state or a card from the inbox. Two things are added, and one is worth saying plainly.

- Workers a lead spawned nest under the lead, the way subagents nest under a parent today. A human's own sessions sit exactly where they always did.
- Cards gain a chip naming the lead they report to, and the inbox gains one filter, by lead.
- Lead sessions themselves are high churn: dormant, working, dormant, many times an hour. They file as stashed and hidden by default, reachable from the tree and the lead page, and surface in the inbox only when blocked. Reviewer sessions nest under the lead that provisioned them and are tidied on verdict.

The organization is a new way in, not a replacement for the way people already have.
