## 4. The human experience

This chapter is the proposal's answer to "what would it take to make this amazing in practice". Each section is one surface, with a mockup, the interactions it must support, and the detail that makes it feel finished rather than merely present. All mockups use this workspace's real project names and invented events.

### The morning brief

The workspace brief is the front page of codecast for anyone who has an anchor. It replaces nothing. The inbox is one keystroke away. But it is what you see first.

<!-- MOCKUP:brief -->

Details that matter:

- **Decisions first, and they are real controls.** The three cards at the top are the same decision cards as the queue. Answering one here answers it everywhere. The card shows the ladder it climbed. Nothing is a summary of a decision; it is the decision.
- **Every line is a link.** Project names open the project's brief. Ids open their objects. The cost line opens the ledger. There is no sentence in the brief the reader cannot verify in one click.
- **Diff since last read.** A reader who was away sees what changed in the brief since they last opened it, marked in the margin. The brief is versioned, so this is a diff of documents, not a heuristic.
- **Coverage is always visible.** The footer says how many sessions and tasks the brief covers and how many it could not read. Never a bare green check.
- **Keyboard.** <kbd>j</kbd> and <kbd>k</kbd> move between lines. <kbd>Enter</kbd> zooms into the referenced scope. <kbd>1</kbd> to <kbd>3</kbd> answer the decision cards. <kbd>i</kbd> jumps to the inbox. All keys render through the existing keycap component.

### Zoom: the organization as a map

The organization view is a tree you can walk. It replaces neither the inbox nor the task board; it is the third way in, organized by scope rather than by time or by status.

<!-- MOCKUP:zoom -->

Details that matter:

- **Each row is a brief headline.** A lead's row shows the first line of its brief, its next actor, its freshness and the count of things at that level that need a human. A worker's row shows its state line, exactly as the inbox does today.
- **Expand to zoom, select to read.** Expanding a row reveals its children. Selecting a row shows its full brief in the right pane. A worker's pane is the conversation, unchanged from today.
- **The human's obligations escape every fold.** A collapsed project that contains a decision for you still shows a red count on the project row. Collapsing is a display choice; it never hides an ask. Version one required this and the view honours it.
- **Missing levels are drawn, not hidden.** A project with no lead appears with a dotted outline and its tasks and sessions listed raw. The affordance to hire a lead is on that row, and nowhere else.
- **Flat view is one key away.** <kbd>f</kbd> toggles between the tree and the familiar flat session list filtered to the same scope. Nobody is forced to think in trees.
- **Drag to refile.** Dragging a task or plan onto another scope refiles it, using the existing task and plan update mutations. The drop preview shows the new reporting line before the drop. Moves while a worker is running are refused with the reason, per version one.

### Decisions with a recommendation attached

The decision card is the moment where the organization earns or loses trust, so it is the most carefully designed surface in the proposal.

<!-- MOCKUP:decision -->

Details that matter:

- **The ladder is shown.** Who asked, who forwarded, and what each lead along the way recommended. A recommendation from a lead is attributed to that lead by name, never presented as the system's view.
- **The recommended option is marked but not preselected.** The human still chooses. One keystroke selects the recommendation; a different keystroke selects something else. Neither is the default.
- **The delegation control is on the card.** Below the options: "Let the Codecast lead answer questions like this." Choosing it grants the lead that decision class within that scope, and says so in plain words with an undo. This is how trust grows, on the card, at the moment the human has just seen the lead be right. Chapter 5 defines the classes.
- **Protected classes have no delegation control.** A deployment, a purchase, a data deletion, an access change, an external message, an unresolved product choice: those cards never offer delegation. The lead's recommendation still appears, as evidence.
- **Overrides are recorded.** When the human picks against the recommendation, the card asks for one line of why, optional, and records the disagreement against the lead. That record is visible on the lead's page as its track record.

### Talking to a lead

A lead is a session, so everything that works with a session works with a lead: `cast send`, chat mentions, `cast read`. The proposal adds one thing: leads are addressable by scope, not only by session id.

<!-- MOCKUP:chat -->

Details that matter:

- **Mention by scope.** In a channel bound to a project, `@lead` reaches the project lead. In an unbound channel, `@lead` reaches the workspace lead. `@lead codecast` reaches a named project's lead from anywhere. The existing anchor mention machinery routes it.
- **Answers from the brief first, live state second.** A lead answers a question from its brief when the brief covers it, and says "from my brief, written 08:34". When it does not, it reads live task and session state and says so. It never invents a state it did not read.
- **Intake is a proposal, not an action.** "Can we fix the duplicate notifications?" in a project channel produces a proposed task in the thread: title, plan, owner, deliverable, exclusions, estimated cost. Nothing exists until a human confirms. Version one specified this and the mockup shows it.
- **A lead can say "not mine".** A question about another project's work gets a pointer to that project's lead, or to the human if there is none. Leads do not answer for scopes they do not read.

### Hiring a lead

"Add a lead" is the only way a lead comes to exist. It is a short flow and it is deliberately the same flow for every scope.

<!-- MOCKUP:hire -->

Details that matter:

- **Scope is chosen, then shown.** The flow previews exactly which plans, tasks and sessions the lead will read, and how many it cannot read because of audience. The human sees the reading contract before agreeing to it.
- **The standing brief is a text box with a good default.** Codecast proposes a job description from the project's name, its recent activity and its chat channel. The human edits it. It is the lead's instructions and it is theirs.
- **Trust starts at understand.** The first screen offers only the first stage: keep a brief, answer questions in chat, recommend on decisions. Decide and direct are visible, greyed, with the sentence "unlocks from the decision card as you agree with this lead's recommendations".
- **Budget is a number the human types.** Per day, per scope, in dollars, and a concurrency cap. No default amount is prefilled. Version one's rule, kept.
- **Rhythm is one choice.** When should the brief be fresh: a time of day, or continuous. The default for a workspace lead is "before 08:30 local time". The default for a project lead is "continuous, coalesced".
- **A lead can be paused, refreshed or retired from the same page.** Pause stops its wakes and keeps its brief. Refresh replaces its session from its documents. Retire keeps its history readable forever.

### Reviewing work

A worker's completion is a claim. The organization turns that claim into a verified result without the human reading the diff, unless they want to.

- A worker runs `cast task done` with evidence, as today. The task moves to review.
- The nearest lead assigns a reviewer. A reviewer is a fresh session, never the worker, with the task, the acceptance criteria and the diff. It reproduces the check and writes a verdict.
- The lead reads the verdict, closes the task if verified, or sends it back with the unmet criteria. It never closes on the worker's word alone.
- The brief says "verified by review (jx7…)" or "claimed done, review pending". The human sees which.

Independent review is expensive, so the plan lead decides where it is required from the plan's acceptance criteria and the task's effect class. A doc change does not need a reviewer. A schema change does. The human can override either way on the plan.

### Organization health

A page the human reads once a week, and the lead's own page.

<!-- MOCKUP:health -->

Details that matter:

- **Track record per lead.** Decisions answered, forwarded and recommended on. How often the human agreed. Overrides with their reasons. This number is what the delegation control in the decision card is asking the human to trust.
- **Load.** Wake backlog, brief freshness, workers per lead, decisions waiting and their age. A lead with a growing backlog is visible before it becomes a problem.
- **Cost by scope**, with the fraction that could not be priced, per version one's coverage rule.
- **Stalls the organization noticed** and what it did about them.

### Mobile

The mobile app already renders the inbox, decision cards and session transcripts. The organization adds two screens and changes none.

<!-- MOCKUP:mobile -->

- The workspace brief, readable, with its decision cards actionable.
- The organization tree, collapsible, with the same fold rule: your obligations show through.

Hiring a lead and editing its standing brief stay on desktop in the first release. Pausing a lead and answering its decisions do not.

### The inbox does not change

Nothing in this chapter removes a bucket, a state or a card from the inbox. Workers spawned by a lead nest under the lead in the inbox the way subagents nest under a parent today, and a human's own sessions sit exactly where they always did. The inbox gets one new filter, "by lead", and one new chip on a card, the scope it reports to. That is the entire change. The organization is a new way in, not a replacement for the way people already have.
