## 5. Delegation and decisions

### The ladder

Every question an agent cannot answer for itself climbs a ladder. The ladder is the reporting line from chapter 2: worker to plan lead to project lead to workspace lead to human, skipping any level that is missing. Codecast already has the mechanism for a question that climbs: `cast decide`. The proposal changes where a decision is delivered, not what a decision is.

Today a decision goes straight to the human's queue. With an organization, it goes to the nearest lead above the asker. That lead does one of three things within a bounded time.

1. **Answers it**, if the decision's class is one the lead has been granted in that scope. The answer is delivered to the asker exactly as a human answer is today. The decision is recorded on the lead's log with the class, the answer and the reason.
2. **Forwards it with a recommendation**, if the class is not granted, or if the lead is unsure. The recommendation is one option and one paragraph. The decision continues up the ladder, and each lead along the way may add its own recommendation. The human sees all of them, attributed.
3. **Forwards it without a recommendation**, if it cannot form a view quickly. Delay is worse than an absent recommendation. Version one set a route deadline per hop and this version keeps it.

Six classes never stop at a lead. A deployment or production change, a purchase or billing change, a data deletion or migration, an access or auth change, an external message in a person's name, and an unresolved product choice go to the human at once, with the ladder's recommendations arriving alongside if they are ready. Version one named these as protected effects. They stay protected.

### Decision classes

A decision has a class. The asker proposes it; the class is a routing hint and never an authorization, per version one. The classes are few and ordinary.

| Class | Example | Default answerer at stage one |
|---|---|---|
| Implementation approach | Which of two equivalent refactors to use | Human, with the lead recommending |
| Scope clarification | Whether a task includes the test fixture | Human, with the lead recommending |
| Priority and ordering | Which of two tasks to do first | Human, with the lead recommending |
| Retry or abandon | Whether to try a failing approach again | Human, with the lead recommending |
| Review disposition | Whether a review finding blocks completion | Human, with the lead recommending |
| Resource | More budget, another worker, a longer timeout | Human, always, until a budget is granted |
| Protected | The six classes above | Human, always |
| Unknown | The asker did not classify | Human, always |

The first five classes are the ones the human will delegate, one at a time, per scope, as they see the lead be right. Resource decisions stay with the human until the human gives the lead a budget, after which the lead answers them within the budget and forwards only when the budget would be exceeded.

### Trust that grows from agreement

The proposal rejects a permissions page as the way trust is granted. A permissions page asks the human to predict, in advance and in the abstract, which questions a lead will be right about. Nobody can. The organization instead asks the human to grant trust at the moment they have just seen evidence.

- Every decision card with a lead's recommendation carries the control "Let this lead answer questions like this in this scope". Choosing it grants that class, in that scope, to that lead.
- The control appears only after the lead has recommended on at least three decisions of that class in that scope and the human agreed each time. Before that, the control is present but greyed, with the count shown: "2 of 3 agreements".
- The grant is revocable from the lead's page and from any later card of that class, which carries "Stop letting this lead answer these".
- An override on a class the lead is trusted with does not revoke the grant by itself, but it resets the agreement count and appears on the lead's track record. Two overrides in a row on the same class revoke the grant and say so.

The result is that a new lead starts with no authority to decide anything and, within a week of ordinary use, holds exactly the authority the human has watched it earn, and no more. The human never fills in a form. The human answers decisions, which they were doing anyway, and occasionally clicks one more button.

### Recommendations are evidence, not pressure

A recommendation is one option and one short paragraph. It names its sources. It is attributed to the lead by name. The card marks the recommended option but does not preselect it. The human chooses with the same keystroke either way.

A lead that cannot recommend says so and forwards. A lead that recommends on every decision regardless of confidence will accumulate overrides, which lowers its track record, which the human sees. The incentive points toward calibration. The product does not need a rule against overconfident leads because it measures them.

### Budgets

Version one defined the budget contract in full: a limit at every scope, admission of every launch against the whole ancestor chain before the launch exists, settled plus reserved plus requested never exceeding the limit, receipts that settle once, unknown prices that stay unknown. This version keeps that contract entirely and adds only the product on top of it.

- A budget is a number the human types when hiring a lead: dollars per day and a concurrency cap. Nothing is prefilled.
- The lead's brief reports spend against budget for its scope and each child scope, with the unpriced fraction.
- When a lead's request would exceed a limit, it forwards a resource decision with the exact numbers: "Sync reliability would exceed its $60 daily limit by $12 to retry the cursor test. Approve $12 more today, or leave it for tomorrow."
- Budgets cascade downward as ceilings, never as allocations. A project's limit bounds every plan in it; it does not divide among them.

### What the human keeps

The human keeps four things no matter how much trust a lead has earned.

- **Ownership.** Every project, plan and task has a human owner. Leads coordinate; they do not own.
- **Protected effects.** The six classes, always.
- **Their own sessions.** A lead never messages, stops, folds or takes over a session the human started, unless the human filed that session under the lead's scope and said the lead may direct it.
- **Sight.** Every grant, every answer a lead gave, every recommendation, every override, and every dollar, on one page, at any time.
