## 7. Directing work

Directing is the third verb and the last one a lead earns. This chapter describes how a trusted lead starts, briefs, redirects and closes work, using the primitives workers already use, so that a worker cannot tell whether a human or a lead is managing it.

### The worker's contract does not change

A worker is any session bound to a task. Today it claims the task with `cast task start`, posts progress with `cast task comment`, pins its state with `cast state`, asks with `cast decide`, and finishes with `cast task done` and a summary. The proposal changes none of those commands and adds none for workers.

What changes is who reads them. Today a worker's state line is read by the human when they open the inbox. With an organization, it is read by the lead above, continuously, and folded into a brief. The worker's incentives to write a good state line are the same as today, and its audience is more attentive.

<div class="note"><b>Why this matters</b><p>Every session that already runs in codecast, on any backend, is already a compliant worker. The organization can be adopted one lead at a time with zero changes to the sessions underneath. This is the single largest reason to reuse the task binding as the reporting line rather than to invent a new one.</p></div>

### Starting work

A lead starts work the way a human does today, with the existing commands, under the budget contract from chapter 5.

- `cast task start <id> --spawn` claims a task and hands it to a fresh session. The session nests under the lead, because the task spawn function now sets the worker's parent to the lead's session (chapter 6).
- `cast spawn --subagent` for a scout or a consultation that has no task of its own. The lead owns the result and folds it into its brief.
- The plan orchestration flow, implementer and reviewer agents across waves, is what a plan lead runs when a plan is ready. Today a human runs it from a session. A plan lead is the session.

Each start, including a fork of the lead itself, is admitted against the lead's budget before the process exists. If admission fails the lead escalates a resource decision with the numbers, and the brief shows the work as "waiting on budget".

### Briefing a worker

The quality of a worker's output is bounded by the quality of its brief. A lead writes a worker's brief from three sources it already holds: the task's description and acceptance criteria, the plan's decision log, and its own scope notes. The brief is written to the standard the human uses for a good `cast spawn` prompt: the goal, numbered steps, constraints, what done looks like, what to escalate.

The task is the brief's durable home. A lead that discovers a constraint mid task writes it to the task description with `cast task update`, so a replacement worker and the reviewer see it too. The task board stays the truth; the spawn prompt is a rendering of it.

### Noticing stalls

A persistent lead's advantage over a fan out is that it is still there when something goes quiet. A stall is a worker whose work state is working or dormant and whose last transcript message is older than the scope's stall threshold, 2 hours by default (chapter 8). A worker with an open decision, a pending permission prompt or a usage limit park is not stalled; it is waiting, and the brief says on whom. The server arms the stall event on the conversation's message count and last activity time, both existing fields, and clears it when either changes, so it fires once per stall.

On a stall the lead reads the worker's state line and recent turns. Then it does one of three things. It sends the worker the missing context. That message is a turn admitted against the worker's budget and the send pair counter from version one chapter 4. Stall messages are capped at two per task per day, after which the brief reports and the lead stops. It escalates the worker's open question with a recommendation. Or, if neither is within its trust, it reports the stall in its brief with a proposed action. The brief always says which.

A dead worker is a different case. Dead means the daemon confirmed the process stopped. Silence does not, because a session parked on a usage limit or a permission prompt looks dead and resumes later. Once termination is confirmed and the old attempt is fenced through the task claim, as version one chapter 4 requires, the lead starts a replacement from the task record. Two workers on one task and one worktree is the failure this rule prevents.

### Redirecting

A lead redirects a worker by `cast send`, the same interruption a human would use. The rules of that interruption stand: a message is expensive, reading is free. A lead reads a worker's transcript and diff before it messages, and the standing brief in chapter 8 says so. A lead never redirects a human's session without the grant described in chapter 5.

Leads do not message each other as a routine. The brief below is the channel; a lead reads it. A lead to lead message is admitted against the send pair counter and capped per hour, so two leads cannot ping pong.

### Closing work

A task closes on verification, not on claim. The flow is in chapter 4: the server sets a default review policy from the diff and the effect class, a human may change it, an independent reviewer comes from a different delegation root, and a lead's own check is called "checked by the lead". The rule that matters here is enforced, not requested: the server refuses a close from a role credential unless a verdict matches the policy. The evidence is on the task: the verdict, the reviewed revision, the check that was reproduced. `cast task done` by a lead carries the same summary requirement as by a human, and the summary names who checked.

A plan closes when its tasks are verified and its acceptance criteria are met, which the plan lead states in the plan's final comment with links. A project never closes; its lead's brief simply reports no active plans.

### Cleaning up

Workers that finished leave worktrees and sessions behind. A lead tidies with `cast stash` and `cast kill`, on sessions it spawned whose permits have settled, so the human's inbox stays readable. It never runs `cast ws destroy`: version one reserved worktree deletion for humans, and this version keeps that. The brief lists worktrees that are merged and clean as candidates, and the human runs the deletion, or a retention job archives after the policy period. Nothing with a dirty tree, an unmerged commit, a pending approval or an uncertain effect is touched. The brief lists what was tidied and what was left and why.
