## 7. Directing work

Directing is the third verb and the last one a lead earns. This chapter describes how a trusted lead starts, briefs, redirects and closes work, using the primitives workers already use, so that a worker cannot tell whether a human or a lead is managing it.

### The worker's contract does not change

A worker is any session bound to a task. Today it claims the task with `cast task start`, posts progress with `cast task comment`, pins its state with `cast state`, asks with `cast decide`, and finishes with `cast task done` and a summary. The proposal changes none of those commands and adds none for workers.

What changes is who reads them. Today a worker's state line is read by the human when they open the inbox. With an organization, it is read by the lead above, continuously, and folded into a brief. The worker's incentives to write a good state line are the same as today, and its audience is more attentive.

<div class="note"><b>Why this matters</b><p>Every session that already runs in codecast, on any backend, is already a compliant worker. The organization can be adopted one lead at a time with zero changes to the sessions underneath. This is the single largest reason to reuse the task binding as the reporting line rather than to invent a new one.</p></div>

### Starting work

A lead starts work the way a human does today, with the existing commands, under the budget contract from chapter 5.

- `cast task start <id> --spawn` claims a task and hands it to a fresh session. The session nests under the lead in the inbox, exactly as a spawned subagent nests under its parent today, because the lead is its spawner.
- `cast spawn --subagent` for a scout or a consultation that has no task of its own. The lead owns the result and folds it into its brief.
- The plan orchestration flow, implementer and reviewer agents across waves, is what a plan lead runs when a plan is ready. Today a human runs it from a session. A plan lead is the session.

Each start is admitted against the lead's budget before the process exists. If admission fails the lead forwards a resource decision with the numbers, and the brief shows the work as "waiting on budget".

### Briefing a worker

The quality of a worker's output is bounded by the quality of its brief. A lead writes a worker's brief from three sources it already holds: the task's description and acceptance criteria, the plan's decision log, and its own scope notes. The brief is written to the standard the human uses for a good `cast spawn` prompt: the goal, numbered steps, constraints, what done looks like, what to escalate.

The task is the brief's durable home. A lead that discovers a constraint mid task writes it to the task description with `cast task update`, so a replacement worker and the reviewer see it too. The task board stays the truth; the spawn prompt is a rendering of it.

### Noticing stalls

A persistent lead's advantage over a fan out is that it is still there when something goes quiet. The rhythm contract in chapter 8 wakes a lead when a worker under it changes state. It also wakes the lead on a timer if a worker has not changed state for longer than the lead's stall threshold, which the human sets per scope and which defaults to two hours for a task in progress.

On a stall the lead reads the worker's state line and recent turns and does one of four things: sends the worker a message with the missing context, forwards the worker's open decision up the ladder with a recommendation, replaces the worker from the task record if the session is dead, or reports the stall in its brief with a proposed action if none of those is within its trust. The brief always says which.

### Redirecting

A lead redirects a worker by `cast send`, the same interruption a human would use. The rules of that interruption stand: a message is expensive, reading is free. A lead reads a worker's transcript and diff before it messages, and the standing brief in chapter 8 says so.

A lead never redirects a session the human started unless that session is filed under the lead's scope and the human granted direction for it. Human sessions inside a project are visible to the project lead's brief and untouchable by its hands.

### Closing work

A task closes on verification, not on claim. The flow is in chapter 4. The rule that matters here is that a lead closes only what it has evidence for, and the evidence is on the task: the reviewer's verdict, the reviewed revision, the check that was reproduced. `cast task done` by a lead carries the same summary requirement as by a human, and the summary names the reviewer.

A plan closes when its tasks are verified and its acceptance criteria are met, which the plan lead states in the plan's final comment with links. A project never closes; its lead's brief simply reports no active plans.

### Cleaning up

Workers that finished leave worktrees, sessions and labels behind. A lead tidies them with the existing verbs: `cast stash` for finished workers so the human's inbox stays readable, `cast kill` for sessions that are truly done, `cast ws destroy` for merged worktrees. Version one's retention rule applies: nothing with a dirty tree, an unmerged commit, a pending approval or an uncertain effect is cleaned up automatically. The brief lists what was tidied and what was left and why.
