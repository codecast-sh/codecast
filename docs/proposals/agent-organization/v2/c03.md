## 3. Understanding at every level

### The brief is the product

The brief is the live account a lead keeps of its scope. It is the single most important object in this proposal, and the one version one left almost undesigned. A lead that keeps a good brief is useful before it is trusted to decide anything. A lead that keeps a bad brief is dangerous no matter what else it can do, because everything above it reads the brief and believes it.

A brief is a document, stored with `cast doc`, owned by the role, rewritten by the lead's current session, versioned, and readable by everyone who may read the scope. It is not a chat message and it is not a transcript summary. It is written to be read by the level above, which means it is written for a reader who has less context and less time than the writer.

The brief answers five questions in a fixed order. The order is the reading order of someone in a hurry.

| Question | What the lead writes | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| What needs a human? | Decisions that climbed to this level and could not be answered here. Each names the asker, the recommendation, and why it is not the lead's to make. | The decision ladder, chapter 5 |
| What changed? | Since the last brief: what finished, what started, what stalled, what was verified, what was decided below. | Task transitions, session state lines, task comments, plan decisions |
| Where are we? | Per child scope, one line: state, next actor, freshness. For a project: each plan and each loose task. For the workspace: each project. | The briefs of leads below, or raw task and session state where no lead exists |
| What is at risk? | Stalls, repeated failures, budget pressure, unverified claims of completion, work with no owner. | Patterns across the child scopes, which only a persistent lead can see |
| What does it cost? | Spend this period by child scope, against the budget, with coverage: how much activity could not be priced. | Usage receipts and the budget contract from version one, chapter 5 |

Each section is short. A workspace brief for eight projects should fit on one screen. A project brief should fit on one screen. If it does not fit, the lead is reporting instead of understanding.

### Every claim carries its source

A brief is only trustworthy if the reader can get from any sentence to the thing it describes in one click. The proposal makes that a hard rule of the format, not a style preference.

Every factual line in a brief carries at least one source reference: a task id, a session id with a message line, a decision id, a plan id, a doc id, a chat message, or a commit. Codecast already renders short ids as live pills anywhere they appear, so the brief uses them as they are. A line without a source is rendered differently, as the lead's opinion, and the reader can see the difference at a glance.

<div class="note"><b>Example of one line in a project brief</b><p>Sync reliability <span class="tag" data-level="plan">plan</span> is stalled on a flaky cursor test. The worker (jx7a2f1) has retried twice and asked for a decision (ct-49102). I recommend skipping the test and filing a follow up; the plan lead agrees. Cost so far this week: $14 of $60.</p><p>Each id above is a live reference. The reader clicks the session to see the retries, the task to see the ask, and the plan to see its acceptance criteria. The cost line links to the usage ledger for the plan.</p></div>

A brief also states what it could not see. If a lead's audience excludes a private session inside its scope, the brief says "one session I cannot read" rather than pretending the scope is fully covered. Version one defined the audience rules. This version makes their consequence visible in the product: coverage is a field on every brief, and the human can always tell the difference between "nothing is wrong" and "I cannot see".

### Understanding flows up, briefs are written down

Each level reads the level below and writes for the level above. That is the whole mechanism. The workspace lead does not read worker transcripts; it reads project briefs. The project lead does not read every diff; it reads plan briefs, task states and worker state lines, and opens a transcript only when something looks wrong.

This is why the organization scales where a flat inbox does not. Adding a project adds one line to the workspace brief, not sixty six cards. Adding a plan adds one line to a project brief. The human reads one screen at the top and zooms only where they choose to.

It is also why honesty matters at every level. A lead that overstates completion produces a brief above it that overstates completion. The product defends against that in three ways. First, the brief separates claimed from verified: a worker's "done" is a claim, a reviewer's check is verification, and the human's acceptance is acceptance. Second, every lead's brief is diffable against its previous version, so a reader can see what changed in the account and not only in the work. Third, chapter 5 records every time a human disagrees with a lead, and that record is the lead's trust score.

### What each level knows

The table below is the reading contract for each level. It says what a lead is expected to have read before it writes, and what it is expected not to read.

| Level | Reads | Does not read by default | Writes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Workspace lead | Project briefs; raw task and session state for projects without a lead; the decision queue; chat channels it is invited to; usage by project | Worker transcripts; private sessions; other workspaces | The morning brief; a weekly published report; answers to workspace questions in chat |
| Project lead | Plan briefs; task tree for the project; worker state lines and task comments; the project's chat channel; plan decisions; PRs and CI for the project's repo | Full transcripts unless a stall or a dispute sends it there | The project brief; task and plan comments; intake proposals in chat; recommendations on climbing decisions |
| Plan lead | Every task in the plan; worker state lines, comments and evidence; diffs for review; the plan's decision log | Transcripts of unrelated plans | The plan brief; review verdicts; plan decisions with rationale; worker briefs |
| Worker | Its task, its acceptance criteria, its code | Anything outside its task | Its state line; task progress comments; a completion with evidence |

The reading rules are enforced by audience, as in version one, but they are also a matter of design. A lead that reads transcripts by default will drown in them and produce worse briefs than one that reads state lines. The contract asks leads to read compressed signals first and raw material only on suspicion, exactly as a good human manager does.

### Freshness and rhythm

A brief has a written time and a source watermark. The product shows both. "Written 6 minutes ago from sources up to 08:34" is a different statement from "written 6 minutes ago", and a reader deciding whether to act needs the second half.

A lead rewrites its brief on two clocks. It rewrites on events: when a decision climbs to it, when a child scope changes state, when a worker finishes or stalls. Those wakes are coalesced so a busy plan does not rewrite its brief forty times an hour. It also rewrites on a cadence the human set when they hired it, so a quiet scope still gets a fresh account before the human reads it. The workspace lead's cadence is the morning brief. Chapter 8 defines the rhythm contract.

### Memory

A lead remembers three things across sessions, all as documents it owns.

- **Its standing brief.** Written by the human when the lead was hired and edited since. The job description: scope, what matters, what to escalate, house rules. Chapter 8 describes it.
- **Its decision log.** Every decision it answered, forwarded or recommended on, with the outcome. Plans already have a decision log through `cast plan comment -d`; the lead's log extends the same idea to the role.
- **Its scope notes.** Things it learned that are not in any task: this test is flaky, that repo needs the platform vendor script, this person prefers short PRs. Written by the lead, editable by the human, read at every session start.

None of those is the transcript. A replacement session reads the three documents and the durable task, plan and decision records and is productive in one turn. Version one's crash recovery rules made this possible. This version makes it the ordinary way a lead starts a session, so the exceptional path and the normal path are the same path.
