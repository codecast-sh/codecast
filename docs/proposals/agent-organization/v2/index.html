01The thesis

In one paragraph

Codecast today has one manager: you. Every session, whether it is a ten minute fix or a week long migration, settles into the same inbox and waits for the same person to read it, judge it, and decide what happens next. This proposal adds the missing middle. Leads at workspace, project and plan scope each keep a live, sourced account of their scope. They answer the questions they have earned the trust to answer. They direct the work below them within a budget you set. They bring you only the decisions that are yours. The result is not a smaller inbox. It is an organization you can read at any level of detail, from one line about the whole company down to one line of a diff.

The problem, measured on this inbox

The numbers below are a snapshot of the inbox this proposal was written in, taken on 5 September 2026 with cast sessions --json and cast task ls --json. They are one person's workspace, not a benchmark.

What Count
Live sessions in the inbox 66
Sessions waiting on the human (needs input) 27
Sessions parked on a machine wake (dormant) 15
Distinct projects those sessions belong to 8
Open tasks across the workspace 522
Tasks in progress right now 95
Active triggers about 90

27 sessions want the same person's judgment. They span 8 projects. The person has to hold all 8 projects in their head to answer any one of them well. Each card was written by an agent that knows its own task and nothing above it. So the human does the compression: they read the card, remember which project it serves, recall what the plan was, judge whether the question matters, and decide. Then they do it again for the next card, in a different project.

That compression is management. Today it runs on one human. It is the reason the inbox has buckets, states, labels, a decision queue and a settle classifier. All of those help the human triage faster. None of them do the triage. The organization proposal moves the compression into the system, one level at a time, and keeps the human at the top of every ladder.

What Monday looks like

The experience the proposal is built around is the first 10 minutes of a day.

  1. 08:40You open the workspace brief.

    One screen written by the workspace lead overnight, sent to you as a direct message with the count of things that need you. What shipped, what is blocked, what changed since you last read. Three decisions that are yours. Cost by project. Every claim links to the task, session, diff or chat message it came from.

  2. 08:42You skim what was handled without you.

    Eleven decisions the leads answered under grants you gave them, two stalls they cleared, one dead worker replaced. Each line has the answer, one line of why, and a key to say you would have chosen otherwise. You press it once. That one override lands on the lead's record.

  3. 08:44You answer two decisions from the brief.

    Each card shows who asked, which lead recommended what and why. One is a deployment. One is a product choice the plan lead could not settle. The third you leave for later. The queue shows it as yours, not lost.

  4. 08:47You zoom into Codecast.

    The project lead's account: three plans moving, one stalled on a flaky test, the stall already assigned to a worker 2 hours ago. You do not open the worker. You could.

  5. 08:49You ask the project lead a question in chat.

    "Is the sync work going to land this week?" It answers from its brief and, where the brief is thin, from the live task tree and the workers' state lines. It says what it does not know.

  6. 08:50You go and do your own work.

    Your own sessions sit where they always did.

Everything in that morning already has a primitive in codecast: anchors, tasks, plans, projects, decisions, chat, triggers, state lines, publish. The proposal composes them into an organization. It adds the few things that are missing. They are leads at project and plan scope, a brief that is a first class object, recommendations on the decision ladder, and a way for trust to grow from evidence rather than from settings.

What version one settled and what this version adds

Version one of this proposal (plan pl-519, published 4 September 2026) was written as a control plane specification. It settled the hard safety questions and the answers stand. In particular: a role is durable and its session is temporary; original spawn provenance never changes; every managed launch is admitted against a budget before it exists; six classes of effect always reach a human; leads read only what their audience may read; a lead can crash and be replaced from durable records without a handoff document. Chapter 11 keeps those contracts and points at the version one chapters that define them.

Version one specified the surfaces as field lists, copy and states, the digest as a data contract, and a first 10 minutes scenario. It did not design the reading experience: the shape of a brief and its reading order, the morning brief, the tree, how trust moves from the human to a lead at the moment of a decision, and the chain between levels. It also chose to start from observation of selected tasks and to defer plan leads to its last gate. This version designs the parts that were missing. Where it changes a version one decision it says so, with the reason, in chapter 12.

Scope of this document

This is a proposal. It authorizes nothing. It does not deploy a feature, spend money, adopt existing sessions or change anyone's permissions. Examples use real project names from this workspace to make them concrete, and invented events to make them readable. Measured numbers are labeled as measured.

02The model

Four levels, and the humans who own them

The organization has four levels of work and, at the top of every ladder, a responsible human. Each level already exists in codecast as a work item. The proposal gives three of them a standing agent, and it leaves the fourth to the workers who already do it.

Workspaceteam or personal

WhoThe workspace lead. This is the existing anchor, the standing agent member a team or a person already has today.

UnderstandsWhich projects are moving, which are stalled, what needs you today, what it all costs. Writes the morning brief.

Project

WhoA project lead. New. One per project that a human chooses to give one.

UnderstandsThe project's plans and loose tasks, its priorities, its risks, its chat channel, what shipped and what is verified. Answers "how is Codecast doing".

Plan

WhoA plan lead. New. Optional. Used when a plan is large enough to need a coordinator of its own.

UnderstandsOne bounded outcome: its tasks, their order, their evidence, the decisions taken along the way. Reviews workers' results against the plan's acceptance criteria.

Task

WhoA worker. This is any session bound to a task with cast task start, exactly as today. Human sessions and agent sessions alike.

UnderstandsOne task: the code, the diff, the tests, the acceptance criteria. Reports up with a state line and evidence.

A lead is a role, not a session. The role is durable. It has a scope, a standing brief, a set of things it is trusted to decide, a budget, and a rhythm. The session that currently holds the role is temporary. It can be replaced, restarted or rotated without the role losing its history or its obligations. Version one established this separation and this version keeps it.

Humans are not a level. Every scope has a responsible human: a project's owner, a plan's owner, a task's assignee. In a team workspace there are several, and each is the top of the ladder for the work they own. A lead never owns a task, a plan or a project. A named person does. A lead coordinates on that person's behalf, within what they have granted. Chapter 4 describes how a brief and a queue serve several people.

Levels can be missing

An organization does not need every level filled. The proposal treats a missing level as the normal case, not an error state.

Shape What happens
A project with a lead and no plan leads The project lead coordinates every plan and every loose task in the project directly. This is the expected starting shape.
A plan with a lead inside a project with no lead The plan lead reports to the workspace lead, or to the human if there is none.
A project with no lead at all Nothing changes. Its sessions and tasks behave exactly as today. The workspace brief lists the project from raw task and session state and says so.
A workspace with no anchor Project leads report to the human directly. The morning brief does not exist until the human creates an anchor.
A task with no plan and no project It is loose work. The workspace lead sees it. No lead directs it unless a human files it somewhere.

The reporting line for any work item is computed from where it is filed, never from who launched it. A task in a plan reports to the plan lead if one exists, else to the project lead of the plan's project, else to the workspace lead, else to the human. Refiling a task moves its reporting line. Who originally spawned the session that works on it never changes and never grants authority.

What a lead does

Every lead does three things, at its own level, and nothing else.

Understand

Keep a live account of its scope, written for the level above. Sourced from task states, session state lines, diffs, decisions, chat and the briefs of the leads below it. Honest about coverage and freshness.

Decide

Answer questions from below that fall within what it has been trusted with. Forward the rest upward with a recommendation attached. Never answer a question that belongs to a human.

Direct

Start, brief, redirect and review the work below it, within the budget and the allowed effects the human granted. Notice stalls. Assign reviewers. Close what is verified.

And not

A lead does not write code, does not own work, does not direct a human's session, does not spend outside its budget, and does not hide anything from the human that the human asked to see.

The three verbs are ordered. A lead that only understands is already useful: that is the first rollout stage. A lead that also decides removes a class of interruption. A lead that also directs removes a class of work. Trust moves down that list one step at a time, per lead, and it moves because the human has seen the lead be right, not because a setting was flipped. Chapter 5 defines that mechanism.

One organization, not a fleet

Codecast already has ways to run many agents at once: cast spawn fan outs, subagent rows nested under a parent, plan orchestration with implementer and reviewer agents, and labels for filing a fleet. Those are batches. They have a start and an end and an orchestrator that goes away.

An organization is different in one way that matters: it persists. A project lead is there on Tuesday because it was there on Monday. It remembers what it decided last week and why. It notices that the same flaky test has stalled three plans. A fan out cannot notice that, because nothing lives long enough to.

The proposal does not replace batches. A lead uses them. When a plan lead needs five tasks done it spawns five workers and files them under itself, exactly as a human orchestrator does today. The organization is the layer that remembers between batches.

Naming

The proposal uses lead for the role at workspace, project and plan scope, worker for a session bound to a task, and brief for the live account a lead keeps of its scope. The existing anchor becomes the workspace lead. The word anchor stays in the CLI for compatibility. No new vocabulary is needed to describe the whole design.

03Understanding at every level

The brief is the product

The brief is the live account a lead keeps of its scope. It is the single most important object in this proposal. Version one defined the fields of a digest, its watermark, coverage, claims and limitations, but not its shape or its reading order. A lead that keeps a good brief is useful before it is trusted to decide anything. A lead that keeps a bad brief is dangerous no matter what else it can do, because everything above it reads the brief and believes it.

A brief is a document, stored with cast doc, owned by the role, rewritten by the lead's current session, versioned, and rendered for each reader from what that reader may see. It is not a chat message and it is not a transcript summary. It is written to be read by the level above, which means it is written for a reader who has less context and less time than the writer. A cadence rewrite of a project brief, or any rewrite whose "What changed" section is not empty since the last post, also posts a project update of the digest kind, which projects already support for agent authors. So the project timeline carries the history without flooding, and people who read updates see it where they already look.

The brief answers six questions in a fixed order. The order is the reading order of someone in a hurry.

Question What the lead writes Where it comes from
What needs a human? Decisions at this level whose holder is a human. Each names the asker, the recommendation, and why it is not the lead's to make. The decision ladder, chapter 5
Handled without you Decisions leads below answered under a grant, stalls acted on, dead workers replaced, tasks closed on review. Each with one line of why and a link. A count: "11 decisions answered by leads, 0 overridden; you would have seen 14 cards." Every item carries "disagree"; items that can still be acted on also carry "reopen" (chapter 5). Decision answers, stall events, replacement events, task history
What changed? Since the last brief: what finished, what started, what stalled, what was checked, what was decided below. Task transitions, session state lines and insights, task comments, plan decisions
Where are we? Per child scope, one line: state, next actor, freshness. For a project: each plan and each loose task. For the workspace: each project. The briefs of leads below, or raw task and session state where no lead exists
What is at risk? Stalls, repeated failures, budget pressure, unverified claims of completion, work with no owner. Patterns across the child scopes, which only a persistent lead can see
What does it cost? Spend this period by child scope and by the lead itself, against the budget, with the fraction that could not be priced. Usage receipts and the budget contract, version one chapter 5

Each section is short. A workspace brief for 8 projects fits on one screen. A project brief fits on one screen. If it does not fit, the lead is reporting instead of understanding.

"Handled without you" is the section that earns the reader's return on day 30. It shows the work the human did not have to do, and lets them spot check it. Reading the brief is also auditing the leads.

Every claim carries its source and its audience

A brief is only trustworthy if the reader can get from any sentence to the thing it describes in one click. The proposal makes that a hard rule of the format, not a style preference.

Every factual line in a brief carries at least one source reference: a task id, a session id with a message line, a decision id, a plan id, a doc id, a chat message, or a commit. Codecast already renders short ids as live pills anywhere they appear, so the brief uses them as they are. A line without a source is rendered differently, as the lead's opinion, and the reader can see the difference at a glance.

Every claim also carries the audience of its sources: who may read the session, task or decision it came from. The brief is rendered per reader. A claim whose sources a reader may not see is dropped from their rendering, and their coverage line says how many claims were dropped. The lead's own prompt is fed only sources at the audience the brief declares, so private text never enters a model in the hope that the output will omit it. The lead's conversation has the same visibility as that declared audience, so reading the lead's transcript reveals nothing the brief would not. Cost totals are computed by the server with the financial read authority version one chapter 3 required and written into the brief; they never enter the model's context. The state line and the direct message headline are written at the widest audience. A personal workspace brief is readable by its responsible human only.

Example of one line in a project brief

Sync reliability plan is stalled on a flaky cursor test. The worker (jx7a2f1) has retried twice and asked for a decision (cd-1042). I recommend skipping the test and filing a follow up; the plan lead agrees. Cost so far this week: $14 of $60.

Each id above is a live reference. The reader clicks the session to see the retries, the decision to see the ask, and the plan to see its acceptance criteria. The cost line links to the usage ledger for the plan.

A brief also states what it could not see. If a lead may not read a session inside its scope, the brief says "one session I cannot read" rather than pretending the scope is fully covered. Coverage is a required field of every brief, and a brief without it cannot be saved. The human can always tell the difference between "nothing is wrong" and "I cannot see".

What is in a scope

A session is in a lead's scope when it is bound to a task or plan filed in that scope. A session running in the same repository with no binding is loose work: the workspace brief lists it, it counts toward nobody's coverage, and no lead directs it until a human files it. Coverage counts are sessions and tasks in scope that the lead read against sessions and tasks in scope, for the reader's audience.

Understanding flows up, briefs are written down

Each level reads the level below and writes for the level above. That is the whole mechanism. The workspace lead does not read worker transcripts; it reads project briefs. The project lead does not read every diff; it reads plan briefs, task states, worker state lines and session insights, and opens a transcript only when something looks wrong.

This is why the organization scales where a flat inbox does not. Adding a project adds one line to the workspace brief, not 66 cards. Adding a plan adds one line to a project brief. The human reads one screen at the top and zooms only where they choose to.

It is also why honesty matters at every level. A lead that overstates completion produces a brief above it that overstates completion. The product defends against that in three ways. First, the brief separates three words: a worker's "done" is a claim, an independent reviewer's check is verification, and the human's acceptance is acceptance. Second, every brief is diffable against its previous version, so a reader can see what changed in the account and not only in the work. Third, the product records every time a human disagrees with a lead (chapter 5), and that record is the lead's track record.

What each level knows

The table below is the reading contract for each level. It says what a lead is expected to have read before it writes, and what it is expected not to read.

Level Reads Does not read by default Writes
Workspace lead Project briefs; raw task and session state for projects without a lead; the decision queue; chat channels it is a member of; usage by project Worker transcripts; private sessions; other workspaces The morning brief; a weekly published report; answers to workspace questions in chat
Project lead Plan briefs; task tree for the project; worker state lines, task comments and session insights; the project's chat channel; plan decisions; PRs and CI for the project's repo Full transcripts unless a stall or a dispute sends it there The project brief, also posted as a project update; task and plan comments; intake proposals in chat; recommendations on climbing decisions
Plan lead Every task in the plan; worker state lines, comments and evidence; diffs on review; the plan's decision log Transcripts of unrelated plans The plan brief; checks of worker claims; plan decisions with rationale; worker briefs
Worker Its task, its acceptance criteria, its code Anything outside its task Its state line; task progress comments; a completion with evidence

Codecast already writes a session insight for every session that settles: a headline, what changed, an outcome, blockers and a next action. Task context already folds a linked session's insight back into the task. A lead reads those insights first. They are the compressed signal the brief is made from, and they already exist.

The reading rules are enforced by the audience rules, which say who may read each session, task and decision. They are also a matter of design. A lead that reads transcripts by default will drown in them and produce worse briefs than one that reads state lines. The contract asks leads to read compressed signals first and raw material only on suspicion, exactly as a good human manager does.

Freshness and rhythm

A brief has a written time and a source watermark. The product shows both. "Written 6 minutes ago from sources up to 08:34" is a different statement from "written 6 minutes ago", and a reader deciding whether to act needs the second half.

A lead rewrites its brief on two clocks. It rewrites on events: when a decision climbs to it, when a child scope changes state, when a worker finishes or stalls. Those wakes are coalesced, default 5 minutes (chapter 8), so a busy plan does not rewrite its brief 40 times an hour. It also rewrites on a cadence the human set when they hired it, from records and never from the previous brief, so a quiet scope still gets a fresh account before the human reads it. The workspace lead's cadence is the morning brief.

Memory

A lead remembers two things across sessions, both as documents it owns, and reads a third that codecast derives.

  • Its standing brief. Written by the human when the lead was hired and edited since. The job description: scope, what matters, what to escalate, house rules. Chapter 8 describes it.
  • Its scope notes. Things it learned that are not in any task: this test is flaky, that repo needs the platform vendor script, this person prefers short PRs. Written by the lead, editable by the human, read at every session start. Capped in size; the cadence wake prunes what is stale, and a human's edit always wins over the lead's.
  • Its decision record. Every decision it answered or recommended on, with the outcome. Codecast derives it from decision hops and answers; the lead reads it and never writes it.

None of those is the transcript. A replacement session reads the documents and the durable task, plan and decision records and is productive in one turn. Version one's crash recovery rules made this possible. This version makes it the ordinary way a lead starts a session, so the exceptional path and the normal path are the same path.

Brief versions pile up on a continuous lead. Codecast keeps every version for 7 days, then one snapshot per day. Briefs, scope notes and decision records are indexed by cast search and cast ask with the lead as author, so "what did the Codecast lead say about the cursor test two weeks ago" is a search, not an ask wake.

04The human experience

Six surfaces make the organization usable: the morning brief, the tree, the decision card, chat, the hire flow and the health page. Each section below is one of them, with a mockup, the interactions it must support, and the details that make it feel finished rather than merely present. Mockups use this workspace's real project names and invented events.

The morning brief

The workspace brief is the front page of codecast for anyone who has an anchor. It replaces nothing. The inbox is one keystroke away. But it is what you see first.

Details that matter:

  • Decisions first, and they are real controls. The cards at the top are the same decision cards as the queue. Answering one here answers it everywhere. The card shows the ladder it climbed. Nothing is a summary of a decision; it is the decision.
  • Handled without you is the second thing you read. Every decision a lead answered, every stall it fixed, every dead worker it replaced. Each carries d, disagree, which records an override against the lead. Items that can still be acted on also carry r, reopen, which puts the decision back in your hands and tells the worker. The count says how many cards you did not see.
  • Every line is a link. Project names open the project's brief. Ids open their objects. The cost line opens the ledger. There is no sentence in the brief the reader cannot verify in one click.
  • Diff since you last read. The brief remembers each reader's last read version, so "show changes" marks what changed for you, not since the previous version.
  • Coverage is always visible. The footer says how many sessions and tasks the brief covers, how many it could not read, and how many claims were dropped for this reader. Never a bare green check.
  • Delivery. At cadence time the workspace lead sends each responsible human one direct message with the "Needs you" count and the headline, using the anchor's existing direct message verb; on mobile that arrives as a push. A brief that is not ready by its cadence shows the previous version with its watermark and a note.
  • Keyboard. j and k move between lines. Enter zooms into the referenced scope. On a decision card 1 to 3 select an option and Enter submits; a protected class asks once more. i jumps to the inbox. All keys render through the existing keycap component.

For several humans

A workspace belongs to a team, so the brief has several readers and the ladder has several tops.

  • Every scope has a responsible human. Plans already have an owner; projects gain one, set at hire time for a scope with a lead and editable on the page. A task's responsible human is its assignee, else its plan's owner, else its project's owner. A decision a lead asks for itself is held by its scope's responsible human, not by the lead's host.
  • Decisions climb to the responsible human of the asker's task. "Needs you" is per reader: Ashot's brief shows "Needs you" and, below it, "Needs Maya". The responsible human, the scope's owner, a team admin, or a lead with a grant may answer. The first answer to commit wins and the other party sees who answered, as the decision record already does today.
  • The brief is one document, rendered per reader. Chapter 3 defines the per claim audience. Two people reading the same project brief may see different lines and different coverage.
  • Grants belong to the scope. The grant control renders only for the scope's owner or a team admin; agreements and overrides count from any human permitted to answer. Hiring, pausing and retiring a lead are the scope owner's or an admin's.
  • The first line of a team brief names its readers. "For Ashot and Maya" is written by the lead from the scope's owners and assignees, so nobody wonders whether a line addressed to "you" means them.

Zoom: the organization as a map

The organization view is a tree you can walk. It replaces neither the inbox nor the task board; it is the third way in, organized by scope rather than by time or by status. It grows from the existing fleet board, which already shows agents by plan and wave.

Details that matter:

  • Each row is a brief headline. A lead's row shows the first line of its brief, its next actor, its freshness and the count of things at that level that need a human. A worker's row shows its state line, exactly as the inbox does today.
  • Expand to zoom, select to read. Expanding a row reveals its children. Selecting a row shows its full brief in the right pane. A worker's pane is the conversation, unchanged from today.
  • The human's obligations escape every fold. A collapsed project that contains a decision for you still shows a red count on the project row. Collapsing is a display choice; it never hides an ask. The inbox already lifts a parent into the questions bucket when a child has an open decision, and the tree uses the same rule.
  • Missing levels are drawn, not hidden. A project with no lead appears with a dotted outline and its tasks and sessions listed raw. The control to hire a lead is on that row, and nowhere else.
  • Flat view is one key away. f toggles between the tree and the familiar flat session list filtered to the same scope. Nobody is forced to think in trees.
  • Drag to refile. Dragging a task or plan onto another scope refiles it, using the existing task and plan update mutations. The drop preview shows the new reporting line before the drop. A move between lead scopes is a human action: the server refuses it from a role credential and from the task's own claimant session, so a worker cannot shop for a lead with a friendlier grant. The pilot refuses moves while a worker is running, with the reason, and the reporting chip shows a pending move.

Decisions with a recommendation attached

The decision card is the moment where the organization earns or loses trust, so it is the most carefully designed surface in the proposal.

Details that matter:

  • The ladder is shown. Who asked, who recommended, who concurred, and when. A recommendation from a lead is attributed to that lead by name, never presented as the system's view.
  • The recommended option is marked but not preselected. The human still chooses. One keystroke selects the recommendation; a different keystroke selects something else. Neither is the default. No option is styled as primary.
  • The delegation control is on the card. Below the options: "Let the Codecast lead answer questions like this." It names the lead that originated the recommendation and shows the agreement count. Choosing it grants the lead that decision class within that scope, with an undo. This is how trust grows, on the card, at the moment the human has just seen the lead be right. Chapter 5 defines the counting.
  • Protected classes have no delegation control, show the bound operation, target and revision, and ask for a second confirmation. The leads' recommendations still appear, as evidence.
  • Overrides are recorded. When the human picks against a recommendation, the card asks for one line of why, optional, and records the disagreement against the lead that made it. That record is visible on the lead's page.

Talking to a lead

A lead is a session, so everything that works with a session works with a lead: cast send, chat mentions, cast read. The proposal adds one thing: leads are addressable by scope, not only by session id.

Details that matter:

  • Mention by scope. In a channel bound to a project, @lead reaches the project lead. In an unbound channel, @lead reaches the workspace lead. @lead codecast reaches a named project's lead from anywhere. The existing channel to anchor mapping and mention machinery route it. A channel can be bound to a lead only if the lead's bot user is a member of it.
  • Answers from the brief first, live state second. A lead answers a question from its brief when the brief covers it, and says "from my brief, written 08:34". When it does not, it reads live task and session state and says so. It never invents a state it did not read. Its answers in a channel are filtered to what that channel's members may read.
  • Intake is a proposal, not an action. "Can we fix the duplicate notifications?" in a project channel produces a proposed task in the thread: title, plan, owner, deliverable, exclusions, estimated cost. Nothing exists until a human confirms. "Create only" needs task write access in the project. "Create and start" needs the scope's responsible human, or a member the owner has delegated budget to, and the created operation is keyed to the thread message so a second click creates nothing.
  • A lead can say "not mine". A question about another project's work gets a pointer to that project's lead, or to the human if there is none. Leads do not answer for scopes they do not read.

Hiring a lead

"Add a lead" is the only way a lead comes to exist. It is a short flow and it is deliberately the same flow for every scope.

Details that matter:

  • Scope is chosen, then shown. The flow previews exactly which plans, tasks and sessions the lead will read, and how many it cannot read. The human sees the reading contract before agreeing to it.
  • Responsible human, host and payer. The flow names the scope's owner, the machine or cloud host the lead runs on, and the account that pays. It shows whether that host can issue the per session credential and run the safe mode from chapter 8, which caps the stage. If the host is offline, the brief goes stale with a note and asks fall through to the human.
  • The standing brief is a text box with a good default. Codecast proposes a job description from the project's name, its recent activity and its chat channel. The human edits it. It is the lead's instructions and it is theirs.
  • Trust starts at understand. The first screen offers only the first stage: keep a brief, answer questions in chat, recommend on decisions. Decide and direct are visible, grayed, with the sentence "unlocks from the decision card as you agree with this lead's recommendations".
  • Caps and rhythm. At understand the flow asks for a concurrency cap, a daily turn cap and an hourly wake cap, because that is what bounds a lead before dollar metering exists (chapter 5). The dollar budget is recorded here if the human wants, and required when direct unlocks. Nothing is prefilled. Rhythm is one choice: a time of day, or continuous with the coalescing default from chapter 8.
  • A lead can be paused, refreshed or retired from the same page. Pause stops its wakes and keeps its brief. Refresh replaces its session from its documents. Retire keeps its history readable forever.

The first brief

Within 10 minutes of hire, the lead writes its first brief in the six part shape. Version one's first 10 minutes scenario is the acceptance test. Its three honesty cases are the checks. An empty project yields an intake draft, not invented work. Inaccessible work yields "I could not verify", not a guess. A project with no blocker gets no manufactured task. A lead that creates a task to show activity fails the pilot.

Reviewing work

A worker's completion is a claim. The organization turns that claim into a verified result without the human reading the diff, unless they want to. Three words stay distinct on every surface: claimed, verified, accepted.

  • A worker runs cast task done with evidence, as today. The task moves to review.
  • A human sets the review policy on the plan or the task. The default is independent review for anything touching schema, production, auth, billing or data, and a lead's own check for the rest.
  • An independent reviewer is a fresh session provisioned from a different delegation root than the worker's lead. At direct stage the lead above provisions it against its own budget; otherwise the human control path does, which in the common starting shape of a project lead with no plan leads means the human. Version one allowed only the human control path; chapter 12 records the change. The reviewer gets the task, the acceptance criteria and the diff, reproduces the check, and writes a verdict. Only its check is "verified". Reviewer and critic cost is charged to the reviewed scope and shows in its cost section.
  • Where the policy allows the lead's own check, the lead reads the evidence and reproduces what it can. The brief calls that "checked by the lead".
  • The lead closes the task on a verdict, or sends it back with the unmet criteria. The server enforces this: a close from a role credential is refused unless a verdict matches the task's review policy. The server sets the default policy from the paths the diff touches and the task's effect class; a lead may tighten it and never loosen it; a human may set it either way. The human's acceptance, where the plan asks for it, is a third step and a third word.

The lead's page

Every lead has a page. It is where the standing brief is edited, where grants live, and where the human sees what a lead has been.

Details that matter:

  • The standing brief is editable in place, with the version the current session has acknowledged shown beside it.
  • Scope notes are readable and editable. A human's edit wins over the lead's.
  • Grants sit with the card each came from, its expiry, and renew and revoke controls. Only the scope's owner or an admin sees the controls.
  • Overrides are listed with their reasons, oldest to newest, so a pattern is visible before the second consecutive one revokes.
  • Session history shows each replacement with the reason and the one line reconstruction note.
  • Pause, refresh and retire are here, and only here.

The project page carries a small panel that shows the lead's headline, next actor, freshness and a link to this page. The panel is not a second control surface.

Organization health

A page the human reads once a week.

Details that matter:

  • Track record per lead. Decisions recommended on and answered. How often the human agreed. Overrides with their reasons. Claims sampled against their sources. This is the number the delegation control asks the human to trust.
  • Load. Wake backlog, brief freshness, workers per lead, decisions waiting and their age. A lead with a growing backlog is visible before it becomes a problem.
  • Cost by scope, with the lead's own line and the fraction that could not be priced.
  • Grants, each with the card it was granted from, its expiry, and a revoke control.
  • Stalls the organization noticed and what it did about them.

Mobile

The mobile app renders the inbox and session transcripts today. It syncs decisions but has no screen that shows or answers one. The organization adds three screens and changes none. The mobile meta line names the same readers as the desktop brief.

  • The workspace brief, readable, with its handled section and its decision cards.
  • A decision card that can answer: options with equal weight, the recommendation as a mark, a second confirmation on a protected class. This is new stage one work, listed in chapter 11.
  • The organization tree, collapsible, with the same fold rule: your obligations show through.

Hiring a lead and editing its standing brief stay on desktop. Pausing a lead, answering its decisions, and, at stage three, stopping its running workers with a preview and taking over a review, do not. Version one required mobile intervention before managed work, and this version keeps that in the stage three exit.

What the inbox gains and what it keeps

Nothing in this chapter removes a bucket, a state or a card from the inbox. Two things are added, and one is worth saying plainly.

  • Workers a lead spawned nest under the lead, the way subagents nest under a parent today. A human's own sessions sit exactly where they always did.
  • Cards gain a chip naming the lead they report to, and the inbox gains one filter, by lead.
  • Lead sessions themselves are high churn: dormant, working, dormant, many times an hour. They file as stashed and hidden by default, reachable from the tree and the lead page, and surface in the inbox only when blocked. Reviewer sessions nest under the lead that provisioned them and are tidied on verdict.

The organization is a new way in, not a replacement for the way people already have.

05Delegation and decisions

The ladder is a race, not a relay

Every question an agent cannot answer for itself climbs a ladder. The ladder is the reporting line from chapter 2: worker to plan lead to project lead to workspace lead to human, skipping any level that is missing or paused. Codecast already has the mechanism for a question that climbs: cast decide. The proposal changes who may answer a decision and what is attached to it. It does not change when the human sees it.

A decision is visible in the responsible human's queue the moment it is inserted, exactly as today. Nothing in the organization delays that. What the organization adds is a second thing that happens in parallel. The leads on the ladder read the decision and attach recommendations. A lead that holds a grant for the decision's class may answer it before the human does.

Two separate facts therefore describe every decision. Its holder is who may answer it: the human by default, or a lead with a grant for that class in that scope. Its hops are the recommendations leads attached on the way up, each attributed and timed. The human can always answer, whoever the holder is. A lead that holds the grant can answer first, and the human then sees the decision answered, with the ladder.

A decision climbing to a lead wakes it at once, like a human ask, never through the coalescing window (chapter 8). Each lead on the ladder then has a hop deadline of 5 minutes to attach a recommendation, counted from that wake. Hops run in parallel across the ladder. The deadline bounds how long the card shows "recommendation pending". It never bounds how long the human waits, because the human never waits. A lead that is vacant, paused, parked on a usage limit, or out of turn or wake budget is skipped when the ladder is computed, and the card says so.

Six classes of effect are protected: a deployment or production change, a purchase or billing change, a data deletion or migration, an access or auth change, an external message in a person's name, and an unresolved product choice. A protected decision has the human as its holder from insertion, whatever the asker called it, and no grant can change that. Recommendations still arrive alongside. Two more decisions are always human held, for different reasons: raising any limit is a policy revision with an author and a reason, and a question asked by a session a human started belongs to that human, whatever its class. A decision a lead asks for itself is held by its scope's responsible human.

Who assigns the class

The asker may propose a class with cast decide --class, the one optional flag a worker may learn, and the server assigns it. The server's assignment can only be stricter than the asker's. When a decision carries a concrete action request, the operation and resource decide the class, as version one chapter 5 specified. When it is free text, a conservative classifier reads the question and the options. Any option that names a deployment, a purchase, a deletion, an access change, an external message or a product choice pins the holder to the human. The record carries who assigned the class and the card shows it.

A lead's answer to a free text question grants no capability, but honesty about stages matters here. At stages one and two a worker runs on its host with its host's shell, so the server cannot stop it from acting on a lead's answer. The defenses at those stages are the server assigned class, which pins anything that names a protected effect to the human; every granted answer visible under "Handled without you"; and the sampled cards below. Bound approval through the human control path, which makes a deploy impossible rather than merely unrecommended, arrives with stage three isolation. Labels route; they never authorize.

Trust that grows from agreement

The proposal rejects a permissions page as the way trust is granted. A permissions page asks the human to predict, in advance and in the abstract, which questions a lead will be right about. Nobody can. The organization instead asks the human to grant trust at the moment they have just seen evidence.

  • Every decision card that carries a lead's recommendation offers the control "Let this lead answer questions like this in this scope" to the scope's owner or a team admin. Choosing it grants that class, in that scope, to that lead.
  • The control names one lead: the lead that originated the recommendation the human chose. A lead that merely concurred with a recommendation from below earns nothing and is shown as "agrees" without a count.
  • The control unlocks after the originating lead has recommended on three decisions of that class in that scope, from at least two different askers, and a permitted human chose its recommendation each time. Before that the control is present and grayed, with the count: "2 of 3 agreements". Decisions asked by the lead's own session do not count, and neither does a recommendation that merely repeats an advisory default.
  • A grant expires after 30 days unless the human renews it from the lead's page. Renewal is one click and shows the record since the grant.
  • Any human in the scope may revoke a grant from the lead's page or from any card of that class, which carries "Stop letting this lead answer these".

Overrides after a grant

A grant does not end the evidence. It changes where the evidence comes from.

  • Every decision a lead answers under a grant appears under "Handled without you" in the brief above it, and on the lead's page, with the class, the answer, one line of why, and the link. Each carries two controls. "Disagree" records an override against the lead and asks for an optional line of reason; it is on every handled item, including replacements and closes. "Reopen" is on items that can still be acted on: it puts the decision back with the human as holder and sends the worker a message naming the change, or reopens a closed task with the unmet criterion.
  • In the first week after a grant, every decision the lead answers under it is also shown to the human as an answered card whose only controls are disagree and reopen, so the human confirms the grant with their own eyes. After the first week, one decision in five is shown that way.
  • Two overrides in a row on a granted class revoke the grant and say so. A single override resets the agreement count.

A new lead starts with no authority. It holds only what the human has watched it earn, and the human keeps watching.

Recommendations are evidence, not pressure

A recommendation is one option and one short paragraph. It names its sources. It is attributed to the lead by name. The card marks the recommended option but does not preselect it. The human chooses with the same keystroke either way. On a protected class the card also shows the bound operation, target and revision, number keys select but do not submit, and a second confirmation submits.

A lead that cannot recommend within its hop deadline says so and the card moves on. A lead that recommends on every decision regardless of confidence accumulates overrides, which lowers its record, which the human sees. The incentive points toward calibration. The product does not need a rule against overconfident leads because it measures them.

Budgets

Version one chapter 5 defined the budget contract: a limit at every scope, admission of every launch against the whole ancestor chain before the launch exists, settled plus reserved plus requested never above the limit, receipts that settle once, unknown prices that stay unknown. Budgets live in the append only policy revisions version one defined, so every change has an author, a reason and an effective time. This version keeps all of that and adds the product on top.

  • Before dollar metering exists, three caps bound a lead: a concurrency cap, a daily turn cap and an hourly wake cap, all in the policy revision, plus the anchor's existing daily session cap. The hire flow asks for them at understand. A lead that has spent its turn or wake cap is skipped on the ladder and its brief goes stale with a note.
  • The dollar budget is a number the human types, never prefilled. It is recorded at hire if the human wants and required when direct unlocks. It covers everything the lead causes: its own turns and wakes, its forks, its messages, and the workers it starts. The brief's cost section shows the lead's own line beside its workers, and labels an estimate as an estimate where the account is a subscription.
  • When a lead's request would exceed a limit, it escalates the question with the exact numbers to its scope's responsible human and never answers it: "Sync reliability would exceed its $60 daily limit by $12 to retry the cursor test. Approve $12 more today, or leave it for tomorrow."
  • Budgets cascade downward as ceilings, never as allocations. A project's limit bounds every plan in it; it does not divide among them.

What the human keeps

The human keeps four things no matter how much trust a lead has earned.

  • Ownership. Every project, plan and task has a human owner. Leads coordinate; they do not own.
  • Protected effects. The six classes, always.
  • Their own sessions. A lead never messages, stops, folds or takes over a session a human started, unless that human filed the session under the lead's scope and granted direction for it.
  • Sight. Every grant, every answer a lead gave, every recommendation, every override, and every dollar, on one page, at any time.

07Directing work

Directing is the third verb and the last one a lead earns. This chapter describes how a trusted lead starts, briefs, redirects and closes work, using the primitives workers already use, so that a worker cannot tell whether a human or a lead is managing it.

The worker's contract does not change

A worker is any session bound to a task. Today it claims the task with cast task start, posts progress with cast task comment, pins its state with cast state, asks with cast decide, and finishes with cast task done and a summary. The proposal changes none of those commands and adds none for workers.

What changes is who reads them. Today a worker's state line is read by the human when they open the inbox. With an organization, it is read by the lead above, continuously, and folded into a brief. The worker's incentives to write a good state line are the same as today, and its audience is more attentive.

Why this matters

Every session that already runs in codecast, on any backend, is already a compliant worker. The organization can be adopted one lead at a time with zero changes to the sessions underneath. This is the single largest reason to reuse the task binding as the reporting line rather than to invent a new one.

Starting work

A lead starts work the way a human does today, with the existing commands, under the budget contract from chapter 5.

  • cast task start <id> --spawn claims a task and hands it to a fresh session. The session nests under the lead, because the task spawn function now sets the worker's parent to the lead's session (chapter 6).
  • cast spawn --subagent for a scout or a consultation that has no task of its own. The lead owns the result and folds it into its brief.
  • The plan orchestration flow, implementer and reviewer agents across waves, is what a plan lead runs when a plan is ready. Today a human runs it from a session. A plan lead is the session.

Each start, including a fork of the lead itself, is admitted against the lead's budget before the process exists. If admission fails the lead escalates a resource decision with the numbers, and the brief shows the work as "waiting on budget".

Briefing a worker

The quality of a worker's output is bounded by the quality of its brief. A lead writes a worker's brief from three sources it already holds: the task's description and acceptance criteria, the plan's decision log, and its own scope notes. The brief is written to the standard the human uses for a good cast spawn prompt: the goal, numbered steps, constraints, what done looks like, what to escalate.

The task is the brief's durable home. A lead that discovers a constraint mid task writes it to the task description with cast task update, so a replacement worker and the reviewer see it too. The task board stays the truth; the spawn prompt is a rendering of it.

Noticing stalls

A persistent lead's advantage over a fan out is that it is still there when something goes quiet. A stall is a worker whose work state is working or dormant and whose last transcript message is older than the scope's stall threshold, 2 hours by default (chapter 8). A worker with an open decision, a pending permission prompt or a usage limit park is not stalled; it is waiting, and the brief says on whom. The server arms the stall event on the conversation's message count and last activity time, both existing fields, and clears it when either changes, so it fires once per stall.

On a stall the lead reads the worker's state line and recent turns. Then it does one of three things. It sends the worker the missing context. That message is a turn admitted against the worker's budget and the send pair counter from version one chapter 4. Stall messages are capped at two per task per day, after which the brief reports and the lead stops. It escalates the worker's open question with a recommendation. Or, if neither is within its trust, it reports the stall in its brief with a proposed action. The brief always says which.

A dead worker is a different case. Dead means the daemon confirmed the process stopped. Silence does not, because a session parked on a usage limit or a permission prompt looks dead and resumes later. Once termination is confirmed and the old attempt is fenced through the task claim, as version one chapter 4 requires, the lead starts a replacement from the task record. Two workers on one task and one worktree is the failure this rule prevents.

Redirecting

A lead redirects a worker by cast send, the same interruption a human would use. The rules of that interruption stand: a message is expensive, reading is free. A lead reads a worker's transcript and diff before it messages, and the standing brief in chapter 8 says so. A lead never redirects a human's session without the grant described in chapter 5.

Leads do not message each other as a routine. The brief below is the channel; a lead reads it. A lead to lead message is admitted against the send pair counter and capped per hour, so two leads cannot ping pong.

Closing work

A task closes on verification, not on claim. The flow is in chapter 4: the server sets a default review policy from the diff and the effect class, a human may change it, an independent reviewer comes from a different delegation root, and a lead's own check is called "checked by the lead". The rule that matters here is enforced, not requested: the server refuses a close from a role credential unless a verdict matches the policy. The evidence is on the task: the verdict, the reviewed revision, the check that was reproduced. cast task done by a lead carries the same summary requirement as by a human, and the summary names who checked.

A plan closes when its tasks are verified and its acceptance criteria are met, which the plan lead states in the plan's final comment with links. A project never closes; its lead's brief simply reports no active plans.

Cleaning up

Workers that finished leave worktrees and sessions behind. A lead tidies with cast stash and cast kill, on sessions it spawned whose permits have settled, so the human's inbox stays readable. It never runs cast ws destroy: version one reserved worktree deletion for humans, and this version keeps that. The brief lists worktrees that are merged and clean as candidates, and the human runs the deletion, or a retention job archives after the policy period. Nothing with a dirty tree, an unmerged commit, a pending approval or an uncertain effect is touched. The brief lists what was tidied and what was left and why.

08The lead's contract

A lead is an agent session running a standing brief, waking on a rhythm, with a bounded part of the cast CLI as its hands. This chapter is that contract: what a lead is told, what each wake gives it, when it runs, what it may do, and how it is replaced. It is written at the level of principle. The exact prompt is an implementation detail to be tuned by measurement, and the house rule for prompts applies: state the intent, and escalate to directive instructions only where the principle fails.

The standing brief

The standing brief is the lead's job description. The human writes it when they hire the lead, from a proposed default, and edits it whenever they like. It is a document. The lead reads it at the start of every session and whenever it changes. It has four parts. The default text for each is proposed here as principle, not as final wording.

Who you are. You are the lead for this scope on behalf of a named person. You coordinate; you do not own. The person owns the outcomes. Your reader is the level above you, who has less context and less time than you.

What matters. The human's priorities for this scope in their own words. For a project: what it is for, what good looks like this month, what to leave alone. This part is human authored and the proposed default is a short prompt for the human to fill.

How you work. Understand before you decide, decide before you direct. Read compressed signals first, transcripts on suspicion. Write your brief for a reader in a hurry, sources on every claim, coverage stated. Recommend only when you have a view; forward when you do not. Never answer what belongs to the human. Never message a session when reading it would do. Never spend outside your budget. Say what you cannot see.

What to escalate. The protected classes, always. Anything the human said matters in "what matters". Anything you are unsure of. A stall you cannot resolve within your trust. A disagreement between leads below you.

The existing anchor already has a standing briefing, sent by cast anchor brief, that covers who it is, its routines and how it reaches people. The lead's standing brief is that document generalized to a scope. One mechanism, three scopes. One line of the anchor's current briefing changes: the anchor no longer arms its own routines with cast trigger add, because the role holds them (below).

Every wake is a message injected into the lead's session. The message is the same shape for every wake and it carries everything the lead needs to act without a full scope scan.

Batch A batch id, and the coalesced list of events: each with an event id, kind, source id and source revision. Kinds: task transition, decision asked, decision answered, worker settled, chat mention, task refiled into scope, brief rewritten below, stall, cadence
Decisions For each decision on the ladder at this lead: its id, class, whether this lead holds a grant for it, and the hop deadline
Asks For a chat mention, the placeholder to reply to; for a terminal or session ask, an ask id to reply to
Exit Two commands end the turn: cast org ack <batch>, with --defer <event id> for any event whose external effect has not completed, then a state line

The lead never has to discover its own ids, and it never has to guess which events woke it. A turn ends with an acknowledgment of the batch and a state line. A deferred event is redelivered in the next batch, as version one chapter 4 requires. A turn that ends with no acknowledgment leaves the batch leased; the lease expires after 15 minutes and the whole batch is redelivered.

Rhythm

A lead runs on wakes. It is dormant between them, in the existing sense: parked on a machine wake, filed under dormant, costing nothing. There are three kinds of wake, all carried by the trigger machinery, and the role owns them. A lead does not arm its own cadence or stall timers; the server does, from the role's settings, and replacement retargets them.

Wake Cause What the lead does
Event A child scope changes state; a worker finishes, stalls or asks; a decision it recommended on is answered; a task refiled into its scope; a lead below it rewrites its brief Handles the batch, rewrites the affected parts of its brief, acknowledges, ends with a state line
Decision A decision climbs to it Wakes at once, never through the window, bounded by the wake cap; recommends or escalates within the hop deadline
Cadence The time the human chose: a time of day for a workspace lead, an interval for a busy plan. The role schedules it; it is not a user trigger. Reads its scope from records, never from its previous brief, rewrites the whole brief, acknowledges, ends with a state line
Ask A human sends it a message, mentions it, or runs cast org ask Answers from brief first and live state second, through the reply target the wake names, ends with a state line

The numbers, stated once here and referenced everywhere else:

Setting Default Floor Note
Coalescing window 5 minutes 1 minute Trailing: the wake fires when the window has been quiet, or at the cap
Batch cap 32 events Overflow stays pending for the next wake
Hop deadline 5 minutes Counted from the decision wake; hops run in parallel across the ladder
Wake cap 12 per hour From the policy revision; a worker that floods cast decide cannot spend more than this of a lead's turns
Batch lease 15 minutes A batch not acknowledged by then is redelivered whole
Stall threshold 2 hours 15 minutes Per scope; chapter 7 defines a stall
Human ask immediate Never waits for the window

Three rules keep the rhythm from looping. Events caused by the lead's own writes never wake that lead. Events that arrive while the lead's work state is working are held and batched when its state line lands, never injected into the running turn. A stall event is keyed on the worker's message count and last activity time, armed and cleared by the server, so it fires once per stall and never again for the same silence.

A lead's turn is short by design. It reads, it writes, it forwards, it stops. A lead that finds itself running a long investigation is doing a worker's job and should spawn a worker instead. The standing brief says so.

Writing

A lead writes four things and nothing else.

  • Its brief, in the six part shape from chapter 3, through cast org brief write, which refuses a brief without coverage.
  • Its state line, the first line of its brief, with cast state, so the inbox and the organization tree show the same headline. It follows the acknowledgment of the batch.
  • Comments on tasks and plans in its scope: progress, blocker, review or note on a task, and decision entries with rationale on a plan, so the work items carry the coordination history rather than the lead's transcript.
  • Recommendations and answers on decisions, through cast decide.

Its decision record is not something it writes. Codecast derives it from the decision hops and answers, and the lead reads it, along with each answer to a decision it recommended on, which arrives as an event. That is how it learns whether it was right.

It does not post routine updates to chat. A channel full of lead noise trains people to mute it, and the brief is where the update belongs. It posts to chat when asked, when it proposes intake, and when the human's standing brief tells it to, for instance a weekly note in a release channel.

Replacement

A role replaces its session for three reasons: the session crashed, its context is long, or the human chose "refresh". All three follow the same path. The role's current session pointer moves to a fresh session and the role's triggers are retargeted. The new session reads the standing brief, the scope notes, the latest brief, and the durable task, plan and decision records. Then it rewrites the brief. Generation fencing from version one chapter 4 drops the old session's pending effects. No handoff document is needed, and if the old session wrote one it is linked as supplemental context.

The human sees replacement as one line in the brief: "New session from 09:12. Reconstructed from records. Nothing pending was lost." If something was lost, the line says what.

Host, payer and model

A lead runs on a host, on someone's account, with a model. The anchor row already records the host. The hire flow asks for all three and shows whether the host can issue the credential, which caps the stage. When the host is offline, the brief goes stale with a note and every ask falls through to the human. Leads inherit the existing backend, model and effort controls. A workspace lead that reads briefs and writes one screen a day suits a strong model at low volume. A busy plan lead that reviews diffs may want a different point on the cost curve.

Every cast org read takes --json. The rollout is gated by trust, not by calendar. Each stage unlocks one verb from chapter 2 and each stage has an exit that is measured on this workspace, which is the first customer. Delivery of decisions to the human does not change at any stage; stage one adds recommendations, stage two adds grants, stage three adds direction.

Stage oneUnderstand

Project leads and the workspace lead keep briefs. Decisions reach the human as today, with recommendations attached. Nothing is started by a lead.

Exit criteria

Each stage exits on evidence gathered by using the feature on this workspace for at least two working weeks. These thresholds are proposed defaults. Version one's rule stands: measure a baseline first and revise them from it.

Stage Exit when
Understand Hire to first sourced brief under 10 minutes on the pilot project, and no lead creates a task to show activity. The human reads the morning brief on most working days by choice, measured by open events. Fewer than 1 in 10 brief claims sampled by a critic agent lack a valid source. Brief coverage above 90% of sessions in scope. No brief has rendered a claimed completion as verified.
Decide At least three classes granted in at least two scopes. Override rate on granted answers, read from the sampled cards and the handled controls, below 1 in 10 over the period. If a stage does not exit, the feature stays at the previous stage for that scope, and the human who ran the stage writes the reason on the health page. There is no calendar date by which a stage must ship.

Dogfood plan

This workspace has 8 projects, one anchor, and a human who reads 66 sessions. It is the pilot. The order is chosen so that each step is useful on its own.

  1. Baseline. 7 working days of the instruments below on today's inbox, before any lead exists, so "before and after" has a before.
  2. Workspace lead only. Give the existing anchor a policy revision with turn and wake caps, the safe mode, the brief format, the cadence wake and the morning brief page. Nothing else. This replaces the human's first pass of triage with reading, and the time saved is the first thing the rollout measures. It exercises brief writing on raw task and session state, the hardest case.
  3. One project lead, Codecast. The busiest project, with plans, a chat channel and a repo. Its brief feeds the workspace brief. This exercises the chain.
  4. Recommendations on the ladder. This project's decisions gain recommendations from its lead. Watch the agreement rate.
  5. First grants. Let the Codecast lead answer approach and ordering questions once the card offers it.
  6. One plan lead, on a plan with more than five tasks, at direct. This is the first lead that spawns work.
  7. Remaining projects, one at a time, as their owners choose.

What is measured throughout

  • Time the human spends in the inbox per day, from client events.
  • Decisions per day reaching each human, and their class mix.
  • Agreement and override rates per lead and class, including overrides on handled items.
  • Brief freshness at the moment it is read, and claims dropped per reader.
  • Cost per scope, with the lead's own line and coverage.
  • Every stall, whether the organization noticed it, and how long before it did.

Compatibility and rollback

Every stage is additive. A workspace with no leads behaves exactly as codecast does today. Retiring every lead returns the workspace to today's behavior with the briefs and decision records kept as readable history. Deploy order and the fallback for clients ahead of prod are in chapter 11.

Decisions for the founder

Seven choices in this proposal are matters of product taste rather than evidence. They are listed here so they can be decided without rereading the document, and they are queued as decisions from the session that wrote it.

  1. Name. "Lead" for the role, and "brief" for its account, or a different pair.
  2. Where the morning brief lives. As the front page for anyone with an anchor, or as a tab beside the inbox.
  3. Trust threshold. Three agreements from two askers to unlock a grant, two consecutive overrides to revoke it, 30 day expiry, or different numbers.
  4. Default rhythm for project leads. Continuous with the 5 minute window, or a fixed cadence.
  5. Independent review by default. Required for every task a lead closes, as version one proposed, or by effect class with a human override on the plan, as proposed here.
  6. Lead sessions in the inbox. Stashed and hidden by default, as proposed, or visible like any session.
  7. First pilot project. Codecast, or a smaller one.

12Failure modes and the version one contract

Product failure modes

Version one enumerated 32 failure cases at the level of transactions, credentials and recovery. They stand. This chapter adds the failures that only appear once there is a product on top: the ways an organization of agents can be technically correct and still make the human's life worse.

Failure What it looks like Defense in this design
Brief drift A brief that is fluent, confident and slowly wrong, because the lead summarized its own previous brief instead of rereading its scope Cadence wakes read the scope from records through cast org scope; the previous brief is never an input. Briefs are diffed. A critic agent samples claims against sources weekly and the health page shows the miss rate.
Sycophantic lead Recommendations that agree with whatever the human seemed to want Track record counts agreement, but the sampled claim audit is independent of the human. Overrides are recorded with reasons and reviewed on the health page.
The human stops reading The brief is good, so the human trusts it and stops zooming, and a bad claim goes unnoticed Claimed, checked and verified are three visual treatments on every line. "Handled without you" puts every lead answer in front of the human with a one key override. Coverage is in the footer.
Interruption flood A busy plan wakes its lead continuously, and the lead rewrites its brief so often the workspace lead never sees a stable one Coalescing with a 5 minute default and a 1 minute floor. Brief rewrites carry a watermark, and a parent reads the latest complete version, never a partial.
Runaway direction A trusted lead spawns workers to fix a problem its workers caused, in a loop Admission against budget bounds it in dollars. Causal depth from version one bounds it in generations. The brief must list every start, so the loop is visible on the next read.
Stall loop A lead pokes a waiting worker every 2 hours, each poke spends a turn and restarts the clock A worker with an open decision, a permission prompt or a limit park is not stalled. Stall messages are admitted against budget, counted per send pair, and capped at two per task per day. The stall event fires once per state revision.
Lead ping pong Two leads message each other in a loop, or a brief rewrite below wakes a parent that sends down The brief is the only routine channel between leads. A lead to lead message is admitted against the send pair counter and capped per hour. Events caused by a lead's own writes never wake it.
Decision flood One worker asks cast decide in a loop and spends three leads' turns per question Decision wakes bypass the coalescing window but count against the lead's hourly wake cap; past the cap the lead is skipped on the ladder and the decision still sits in the human's queue.
Acting on advice At stages one and two a worker acts on a lead's granted answer with its host's full shell The server assigned class pins anything naming a protected effect to the human; every granted answer is visible under "Handled without you" with disagree and reopen; sampled cards in the first week; the stage two exit audits option text. Bound approval arrives with stage three isolation.
Trust creep Grants accumulate until a lead decides most things and the human has forgotten what it may do Every grant is on the lead's page with the date and the card it was granted from, and expires after 30 days unless renewed. Every granted answer appears under "Handled without you" with an override control, and a sample still arrives as a card. Two consecutive overrides revoke.
Lead disagreement A plan lead and its project lead recommend opposite things Both recommendations reach the human, attributed. Only the originating lead of the chosen option earns an agreement; the other records an override. Disagreement between leads is itself a reason to escalate rather than answer.
Stale delegation A grant made when a scope was small still applies after the scope has grown Grants are per scope and expire. The scope's membership is shown when a decision is answered under a grant. A refiled task carries no grants with it.
Silent scope A lead cannot read half its scope and does not say so Coverage is a required field of the brief. A brief with unknown coverage cannot be saved. Per reader rendering states how many claims were dropped for that reader.
Cost surprise The human learns at the end of the month what the organization cost Cost with coverage, including the lead's own line, is one of the six sections of every brief, at every level, every day.

Glossary

  • Lead. The role that understands, decides and directs at workspace, project or plan scope. The workspace lead is the existing anchor.
  • Worker. A session bound to a task. Human or agent.
  • Brief. A lead's live, versioned, sourced account of its scope, in six fixed parts, rendered per reader. Version one called this the digest.
  • Standing brief. The lead's job description, written by the human. Version one called this the brief.
  • Ladder. The reporting line a decision's recommendations climb: worker, plan lead, project lead, workspace lead, human, skipping missing or paused levels. The human sees the decision at insertion regardless.
  • Holder. Who may answer a decision: the responsible human, or a lead with a grant for its class in its scope.
  • Grant. Permission for a lead to answer one decision class in one scope, given from a decision card after observed agreement, recorded as a policy revision, expiring after 30 days.
  • Track record. A lead's agreements, overrides and sampled claim accuracy, on its page.
  • Coverage. The fraction of a scope a brief could read, stated on every brief, per reader.
  • Claimed, checked, verified, accepted. A worker's completion, a lead's own check, an independent reviewer's check, and the human's acceptance. Four different words on every surface.
  • Wake. A message that starts a lead's turn: an event, a cadence, or an ask.
