Codecast · product directions · august 2026

You are not browsing documents.
You are running a fleet.

Right now this account has 34 sessions, 30 of them live. The interface treats each one as a document you navigate to — a list, a detail pane, a back button. That framing is why every layout fix has been a fight: we keep tuning columns for a filing cabinet while the actual job is supervising autonomous workers who interrupt you. These four directions each take that job seriously, and each optimizes for something different. They are not variations on a theme — pick by what you want the app to be best at.

Direction 1

Mission Control

The home screen is the fleet, not a conversation.

Codecast opens on a live board of every agent: what it is doing, how long it has been doing it, and whether it is stuck. You scan rather than read. Conversations are drill-ins that overlay the board and return you to it — never a place you navigate away into.

What it optimizes
  • Situational awareness. "What is running?" is answered by looking, not hunting.
  • Blocked work surfaces itself — the amber band is always at the top.
  • Scales with the fleet: 30 agents is the normal case, not the stress case.
Borrowed from
  • Network operations centres, trading desks, flight boards
What it costs
  • Deep reading gets worse — a drill-in is a visit, not a home.
  • Needs genuinely trustworthy state. A board that lies is worse than a list.
Direction 2

The Queue

The scarce resource is your attention, not your screen.

The app hands you exactly one thing that needs a decision, full width, with the context to decide it. You answer; it advances. There is no layout, because there is only ever one thing on screen. When the queue empties, the app says so and gets out of the way.

What it optimizes
  • Throughput on interruptions. 30 agents asking questions becomes a stack you can clear.
  • Zero layout management — the problem is deleted, not solved.
  • Every decision gets the full width for its context.
Borrowed from
  • Superhuman, code review queues, air traffic handoff
What it costs
  • Hostile to exploration — you cannot wander, only advance.
  • Side by side is impossible by construction.
  • Mis-ranking is fatal: the queue's order becomes your priorities.
Direction 3

Workbenches

Stop arranging panels. Name the activity instead.

You never want "a doc beside a session" — you want to be reviewing, or be triaging. Define a handful of workbenches, each a fixed arrangement built for one activity, and switch between them wholesale with one key. No pins, no closes, no per-panel controls: the layout is a property of what you are doing.

What it optimizes
  • Side by side without fiddling — the pairing is designed once, per activity.
  • Switching context is one keystroke and total.
  • Nothing to get into a broken state, because no state is user-assembled.
Borrowed from
  • Blender workspaces, Logic Pro screensets, tmux layouts
What it costs
  • The presets have to be right; a wrong workbench is a cage.
  • Adding a fifth and sixth workbench recreates the original mess.
Direction 4

Canvas

Parallel work is spatial. Give it space.

One infinite board. Every session, task and doc is an object with a place, and related work sits together. Zoom is the only navigation: far out you see status, mid you see cards, close in you are in the conversation. You never open or close anything — you move.

What it optimizes
  • Parallelism. Ten agents on one problem can be one visible cluster.
  • Spatial memory replaces navigation — things stay where you left them.
  • Zoom is a continuous overview/detail control, not a mode.
Borrowed from
  • Figma, tldraw, Muse; semantic zoom from cartography
What it costs
  • Spatial upkeep is real work, and it decays.
  • Weak on small screens; poor for pure keyboard flow.
  • The most unfamiliar of the four — the biggest bet.

How they compare

These are not mutually exclusive, but they are different bets about what the app is for. The honest read is in the table.

If you want my read: the two that fit the evidence are Mission Control and The Queue, and they compose — the board is where you look, the queue is where you act. One answers "what is happening", the other answers "what needs me now". Together they cover the whole loop of supervising a fleet, and neither requires you to arrange anything.

Workbenches is the pragmatic choice if the current shape is roughly right and the problem is only that arranging it is tedious. It is the smallest change that would have prevented every layout fight in this session.

Canvas is the real swing. It is the only direction that treats parallelism as a first-class spatial fact rather than a list that got long, and the only one that would look like nothing else. It is also the one most likely to be abandoned after a month of upkeep.
